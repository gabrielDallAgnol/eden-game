## 1. Taxonomy: what structural kinds of tech tree exist?

### Takeaway
At least eleven distinct structures are in use, and they differ on two axes: who decides what comes next (player choice, chance, or the state of the world) and how visible the future is (fully laid out, partly hidden, or blind). The "fixed tree bought with points" is only one corner of that space; most notable designs since about 2014 have moved toward chance or world-state deciding what is available.

### Cited Findings
- **Origin.** The tech tree idea is traced to the 1980 board game Civilization and was popularised by Sid Meier's 1991 computer game. — [Asterisk, "The Universal Tech Tree"](https://asteriskmag.com/issues/10/the-universal-tech-tree). Soren Johnson likewise says the closest earlier parallel to the Civilization tech tree was the technology-card discounts in the Avalon Hill board game. — [Designer Notes, Old World #4](http://www.designer-notes.com/old-world-designer-notes-4-the-technology-deck/)
- **Fixed directed graph with AND prerequisites (classic Civilization).** Only one technology is researched at a time, so the order chosen "makes a considerable difference in the outcome of the game and generally reflects the player's preferred style of gameplay." — [Wikipedia, Civilization (video game)](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civnet). Johnson's summary of its virtue: "It was always abundantly clear how to discover Gunpowder if you wanted to build Musketeers." — [Designer Notes, Old World #4](https://www.designer-notes.com/?p=1703)
- **Directed graph with AND/OR prerequisites, no era gates (Civilization IV).** Civ IV's tree is described as "very flexible, thanks to the addition of OR gates and the elimination of static eras," so a technology can be reached by researching different techs. — [CivFanatics, Civ IV article](https://www.civfanatics.com/?p=666). By contrast Civ III split research into four eras that had to be completed in turn; Civ IV has "no such limitation." — [GamePressure, Civ IV research guide](https://www.gamepressure.com/sidmeierscivilization4/research/zb254)
- **Era-gated rows (Civilization III, Age of Empires family, Crusader Kings III).** In CK3, innovations sit in four eras, sorted into military, civic or special categories; an era must be reached before its innovations can be unlocked, and the later eras need two extra criteria. — [Paradox forum, CK3 Dev Diary 27](https://forum.paradoxplaza.com/forum/threads/ckiii-dev-diary-27-cultures-cultural-innovations.1391886/); [GameWatcher guide](https://gamewatcher.com/news/crusader-kings-3-culture-and-innovations)
- **Tech web (Civilization: Beyond Earth).** Lead designers Will Miller and David McDonough said they recognised early that a linear tree "felt too certain," so they arranged technologies as a web that suggests several possible futures. — [Space.com op-ed via Yahoo](https://news.yahoo.com/taking-sid-meiers-civilization-beyond-earth-op-ed-173742535.html). The star-like layout was inspired by Amplitude's Endless Space, and the stated motive included that fans "had figured out the critical paths to the tech they needed to win and endlessly discussed them in the forums." — [GamesBeat interview](https://gamesbeat.com/civilization-beyond-earths-space-setting-let-designers-think-outside-the-hex-interview/). McDonough called the web one of the team's "first, favorite, and most fundamental redesigns." — [GameSpot](https://gamespot.com/articles/beyond-earth-takes-civilization-to-the-stars/1100-6418906/)
- **Weighted random card draw (Stellaris).** The dev diary describes research as drawing from a deck "that is very much stacked"; the game has no tech tree "in the classical sense," and each new project offers "three semi-random choices." Some cards sit outside the deck and can only be drawn in special circumstances such as researching anomalies or investigating debris. — [Paradox forum, Stellaris Dev Diary 11](https://forum.paradoxplaza.com/forum/developer-diary/stellaris-dev-diary-11-research-technology.893377/). Game director Henrik Fåhraeus called a tech tree "too predictable." — [PC Gamer](https://pcgamer.com/stellaris-dev-the-galaxy-should-always-be-unknown-and-surprising)
- **Deck-builder draw with discard (Old World).** Each tech is a card; the player draws four from the current pile, picks one and discards the rest; a discarded tech is unavailable until the deck is reshuffled. One-use bonus cards (a free Settler, a Great Scientist, a boost of Stone) sit in the same deck and are "trashed" after use. — [Designer Notes, Old World #4](http://www.designer-notes.com/old-world-designer-notes-4-the-technology-deck/)
- **Random subset per game (Master of Orion, Sword of the Stars).** In Sword of the Stars "each time a new campaign is started, the tech tree is rolled out at random," so key weapons and technologies may be present or absent. — [GamesRadar review](https://www.gamesradar.com/sword-of-the-stars-11/2). Master of Orion is described as having randomised trees where even common techs are not guaranteed to appear. — [StrategyWiki](https://strategywiki.org/wiki/Master_of_Orion/Technology_tree); the Wikipedia tech-tree article instead says the full tree is constant but each player gets a subset depending on race. — [Wikipedia, Technology tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technology_tree) (the two accounts conflict; not resolved).
- **Research funded by output, no research buildings.** Master of Orion (1993), Galactic Civilizations and Sword of the Stars have no research buildings; players allocate a share of each colony's output to research. — [Wikipedia, Technology tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technology_tree)
- **Tiered needs (Anno 1800).** There is no conventional tech tree for buildings. The developers describe residents as the centre of progression: raising the population tier "gives you access to a growing catalogue of new buildings and opens up new and more complex production chains." — [Anno Union devblog, residential tiers](https://www.anno-union.com/en/devblog-residential-tiers/). Five Old World tiers (farmers, workers, artisans, engineers, investors); houses must be full and basic needs met to advance; luxury needs only affect happiness and tax. — [Anno 1800 fan wiki, Population](https://anno1800.fandom.com/wiki/Population?oldid=3313). Higher-tier buildings need far more workers (a potato farm 20 farmers, a steelworks 200 workers). — [PC Gamer tips guide](https://pcgamer.com/anno-1800-tips-guide)
- **Milestone-earned points (Dawn of Man).** Knowledge points come from milestones (population reached, number of clothes crafted, plants farmed, first deer killed, new animal seen, buildings built) and are then spent on a tech tree. — [Twinfinite walkthrough](https://twinfinite.net/guides/dawn-of-man-walkthrough/); [TheGamer beginner's guide](https://www.thegamer.com/dawn-of-man-beginners-guide/)
- **Event-triggered boosts on a fixed tree (Civilization VI Eurekas).** Ed Beach: "for pretty much every technology in the tech tree, we've associated a specific activity that's sort of like a quest." — [TechBloat, quoting a 2016 Beach interview](https://www.techbloat.com/how-civilization-vis-turns-research-and-victory-conditions-work.html) (secondary; verify against the original interview).
- **Diffusion from neighbours (Europa Universalis IV institutions, Victoria 3 spread, CK3 exposure).** See section 8 for the exact mechanisms.
- **Tree folded into another system (At the Gates).** Jon Shafer made professions "the entire tech tree" in At the Gates, inspired by Crusader Kings and King of Dragon Pass. — [PC Gamer feature on Shafer](https://www.pcgamer.com/how-making-a-new-strategy-game-nearly-destroyed-civ-5-designer-jon-shafers-life)
- **Parallel trees by domain.** Civ VI split culture into a separate civics tree researched with Culture. — [Wikipedia, Civilization VI](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civilization_6). Victoria 3 has three trees: Production, Military, Society. — [Paradox forum, Victoria 3 Dev Diary 27](https://admin-forum.paradoxplaza.com/forum/developer-diary/victoria-3-dev-diary-27-technology.1502428)

### Inferences
- Eden's "want plus means" rule is closest to a blend of three of these: Anno's tiered needs (the people's wants gate what exists), Civ VI Eurekas (a world event causes the advance) and Paradox diffusion (it arrives because it is nearby). None of the surveyed games makes the want itself the sole trigger, so Eden has no direct precedent to copy, and no direct precedent that failed either.
- Every randomised design above was introduced to stop players memorising one route. Eden gets that variation without dice, because the map and the god's interventions decide which wants arise first. The Eureka reception (section 4) is the warning: if the trigger for each technology is identical every game, players learn the list and it becomes a chore.
- A second axis worth deciding on purpose is how much of the future the player can see. Classic trees show everything; Stellaris and Old World show three or four options; Alpha Centauri's blind research hides the target.

### Gaps
- **Alpha Centauri blind research.** No source was found for Brian Reynolds's reason for it. From memory, unverified: the player sets weights across Explore, Discover, Build and Conquer and the game picks the technology; it could be switched off in the rules. The search returned only Reynolds's 1999 designer diary on GameSpot, which was not readable here. — [GameSpot, An Interview with Brian Reynolds](https://www.gamespot.com/articles/an-interview-with-brian-reynolds/1100-2564268)
- **Usage-based learning in strategy games.** No design article on "learning by doing" technology was found. The nearest hit discusses veterancy and upgrades in RTS games. — [Game Developer, research and upgrades in RTS games](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/some-thoughts-about-research-and-upgrades-in-rts-games). Treat this taxonomy entry as unsupported.
- The Wikipedia "Technology tree" article and its citation list could not be read directly.

## 2. What does Tuur Ghys's 2012 Game Studies paper conclude?

### Takeaway
Ghys reads tech trees as evolutionary tree diagrams and tests four games against the idea of technological determinism. He finds all four deterministic to a degree, with Civilization IV the most thoroughly so (it wires social and political ideas into the same chain as machines) and Age of Empires the loosest. The tree sizes he reports could not be retrieved in this session.

### Cited Findings
- The paper is "Technology Trees: Freedom and Determinism in Historical Strategy Games," Game Studies vol. 12 no. 1, September 2012. It "deals with the representation of the history of technology in historical strategy games by the use of evolutionary tree diagrams called technology trees, in relation to the concept of technological determinism," comparing Age of Empires, Empire Earth, Rise of Nations and Civilization IV. — [Game Studies 12(1), Ghys](https://www.gamestudies.org/1201/articles/tuur_ghys); [issue page](https://gamestudies.org/1201)
- The working definition of technological determinism used in the discussion: social progress is driven by technological innovation that follows an apparently inevitable course. — [Game Studies 12(1), Ghys](https://www.gamestudies.org/1201/articles/tuur_ghys) (via search summary).
- Rigidity example attributed to Ghys: in Civilization IV a player cannot research Robotics without first having researched Mysticism. — [University of Liverpool thesis, "Playing Games with Technology"](https://livrepository.liverpool.ac.uk/3027693/1/Playing%20Games%20with%20Technology.pdf)
- Ghys notes the "inclusion of social and political principles in the tech tree," with links in which "mechanical" technologies lead to "social" ones. — [ResearchGate, "Teaching with Technology Trees"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/375672860_Teaching_with_Technology_Trees_-_Game-based_Learning_for_History_Education) (secondary citation of Ghys).
- Overlap figures reported from the paper: Civilization IV 34%, Rise of Nations 30%, Age of Empires 39% (despite ending around 500 AD), Empire Earth 17%. The search summaries disagree on what is being compared: one says overlap between civilizations' trees, another says overlap between each game's tree and historical development. — [Game Studies 12(1), Ghys](https://www.gamestudies.org/1201/articles/tuur_ghys) (via search summary; the meaning of "overlap" must be checked in the original).
- Structural contrasts: Empire Earth's effects system is described as "simple and rigid"; Age of Empires' tree "has more loose connections and no social determinism, but is unique per civilization." — [Game Studies 12(1), Ghys](https://www.gamestudies.org/1201/articles/tuur_ghys) (via search summary).
- The Age of Empires manual itself states that cultures had to master the preceding technology to proceed and advance. — [Game Studies 12(1), Ghys](https://www.gamestudies.org/1201/articles/tuur_ghys) (via search summary).
- Ghys expanded the work into a 2014 book, "Moving up the Tech Tree: The history of technology in historical strategy games," which describes three types of technological determinism found in tech trees. — [Amazon listing](https://www.amazon.com/Moving-Tech-Tree-technology-historical/dp/3659487600)
- Later writers apply his frame: "the concept of the tech tree itself (at least, in its most common implementations) embodies a technologically deterministic view of history," and even mesh-like trees such as Civilization's keep many deterministic features. — [Play the Past, "Technoscience in Virtual Worlds"](https://www.playthepast.org/?p=4053)

### Inferences
- The paper's title pairing, freedom and determinism, maps onto a practical design tension: the player is free to choose order, yet every route is laid down in advance and every civilization walks the same ground. Eden removes the choosing, so its answer to determinism has to come from which wants arise where, not from player routing.
- Ghys's sharpest example (Mysticism before Robotics) is a criticism of long AND chains that cross domains. A want-driven model avoids the oddity only if each technology's cause is local to its own domain.

### Gaps
- **Tree sizes are missing.** The number of technologies and ages Ghys gives for each of the four games was not in any search result. The paper is open access; it must be read directly to fill this in.
- Which editions he analysed (base games or expansions) was not confirmed.
- His final conclusion in his own words, including whether he classes the games as "hard" or "soft" determinism, was not retrieved.
- What the three determinism types in the 2014 book are was not retrieved.

## 3. What have notable designers said, and what problems were they solving?

### Takeaway
The designers who moved away from the fixed tree name the same problem in different words: once the whole tree is visible and identical every game, veterans make the same choices each time. The fixes fall into three families: hide or randomise the options (Stellaris, Old World), make the world decide what is cheap (Civ VI), or restructure the whole game into chapters so the lead cannot compound (Civ VII, Humankind).

### Cited Findings
- **Soren Johnson (Civ IV, Old World) on the trade-off.** "The tech tree was a perfect bit of game design, especially if the goal was clarity"; "one of the trade-offs that the traditional tech tree made in the name of clarity was determinism." — [Designer Notes, Old World #4, 18 August 2021](https://www.designer-notes.com/?p=1703)
- **Johnson on why it mattered.** Players learned the exact order of techs leading to a goal, so veterans made the same choices game after game. — [BoardGameGeek design note quoting Johnson](https://boardgamegeek.com/thread/3659774/design-note-technology-as-contested-territory) (secondary).
- **Johnson on fashion.** With the popularity of games built on random runs, fixed tech trees are "going against the grain of contemporary design." — [CivFanatics thread on Johnson's notes](https://forums.civfanatics.com/threads/soren-johnsons-designer-notes-about-the-tech-tree.672513)
- **Johnson on the goal of the fix.** The ideal solution forces hard choices while being transparent about why the other valid options are unavailable; in Old World a discarded tech stays out of reach until the reshuffle, which "creates difficult decisions." — [Designer Notes, August 2021 archive](https://www.designer-notes.com/2021/08/); [Old World #4](http://www.designer-notes.com/old-world-designer-notes-4-the-technology-deck/)
- **Ed Beach (Civ VI) on "active research."** "In the past, pretty much all the Civilization games worked the same way with research. You'd pick a node on the technology tree..." and wait; in Civ VI "You can't just burn through the tech tree the same way in every game." The search summary did not say which outlet carried which line; the 2016 coverage is — [Gamasutra/Game Developer, "Civilization VI's lead designer wants to shake up players"](https://gamasutra.com/design/-i-civilization-vi-i-s-lead-designer-wants-to-shake-up-players); [Time interview](https://time.com/4324490/civilization-6-interview/)
- **Beach on a second optimum he wanted to break.** Players had found that building to four or five cities was the optimal strategy in the previous game. — [Gamasutra/Game Developer](https://gamasutra.com/design/-i-civilization-vi-i-s-lead-designer-wants-to-shake-up-players)
- **Beach on the map.** He likes board games "very focused on positional play and using or creating the map as you're playing through it." — [PC Gamer, The complete history of Civilization](https://www.pcgamer.com/the-complete-history-of-civilization/6)
- **Beach (Civ VII) on the late game.** Fewer than half of players finished a game of Civilization VI; he called the share "depressingly" low. "The number one thing we wanted to do was break the game up into chapters... We're calling them 'ages' this time." — [Game Developer, "Firaxis' big swing with Civilization VII"](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/firaxis-big-swing-with-civilization-vii-convincing-players-to-actually-finish-their-games); [Digital Trends](https://digitaltrends.com/gaming/firaxis-games-civilization-7-finish-games). He told Edge that data had long shown unfinished campaigns to be a major issue and that he had doubted the ages system would work. — [GamesRadar](https://gamesradar.com/games/strategy/civ-7-director-thought-the-new-ages-system-might-not-work-but-says-it-does-fix-the-number-one-issue-players-not-getting-to-the-end-of-their-games). He also said "the first 50 turns of figuring out where you are on the map and who's around you is innately fun." — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/firaxis-big-swing-with-civilization-vii-convincing-players-to-actually-finish-their-games)
- **Henrik Fåhraeus (Stellaris).** A tech tree is "too predictable"; the dev diary gives each project three semi-random choices from a stacked deck, with scientists' specialities steering the odds. — [PC Gamer](https://pcgamer.com/stellaris-dev-the-galaxy-should-always-be-unknown-and-surprising); [Stellaris Dev Diary 11](https://forum.paradoxplaza.com/forum/developer-diary/stellaris-dev-diary-11-research-technology.893377/)
- **Will Miller and David McDonough (Beyond Earth).** A linear tree "felt too certain"; the web moves from a left-to-right path to nonlinear advancement. — [Space.com op-ed via Yahoo](https://news.yahoo.com/taking-sid-meiers-civilization-beyond-earth-op-ed-173742535.html); [GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/civilization-beyond-earths-space-setting-let-designers-think-outside-the-hex-interview/)
- **Paradox (Victoria 3).** Innovation measures how fast a country discovers technology; universities raise it; literacy caps how much can be applied (the diary's example is Mexico, whose universities outrun its literacy); keeping Industrialists, Armed Forces or Intelligentsia content makes the matching tree cheaper; about 175 technologies were planned at release, many countries start with 20 to 30, and leaders find roughly one per year. — [Victoria 3 Dev Diary 27](https://admin-forum.paradoxplaza.com/forum/developer-diary/victoria-3-dev-diary-27-technology.1502428)
- **Paradox (EU4).** The 2016 diary said technology groups and world technological development had barely changed since EU1; institutions replaced them and Westernization was removed. — [EU4 Development Diary, 26 May 2016](https://forum.paradoxplaza.com/forum/threads/eu4-development-diary-26th-of-may-2016.937696/)
- **Amplitude (Humankind).** Fame decides the winner. Era Stars are its main source: seven categories per era, covering economy, military and expansion; a star is worth less Fame the longer it takes; stars reset each era; stars matching the culture's affinity are worth far more. When a rival advances first, the others get "Competitive Spirit" stars that give no Fame but speed their advance. — [PC Gamer, How to earn Fame in Humankind](https://pcgamer.com/humankind-fame-era-stars); [PC Gamer, Fame is key to victory](https://pcgamer.com/fame-is-key-to-victory-in-civilization-challenger-humankind)
- **Sid Meier.** From his 2020 memoir: "It didn't matter that gunpowder, for example, was originally developed for medicinal purposes in China"; what mattered was that a civilization could have discovered it any time after the perfection of iron. — reported in coverage of the memoir; the search result set was [Wikipedia, Civilization (video game)](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civnet) and [PC Gamer's history](https://pcgamer.com/the-complete-history-of-civilization/2) (exact carrier of the quote not confirmed).
- **Jon Shafer.** Published a long retrospective, "Revisiting the Design of Civ 5," in February 2013. — [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/revisiting-the-design-of-civ-5). An older remark attributed to him: Civ needs "something for the late-game builder if they want people to enjoy the entire length of the game." — [CivFanatics thread "Nobody finishes games of Civ!"](https://forums.civfanatics.com/threads/%E2%80%9Cnobody-finishes-games-of-civ.615789/) (forum repost, secondary).
- **General 4X late-game diagnosis.** Late in a game the player has "built enough population, researched enough technologies, and constructed enough buildings and units" that the experience becomes unwieldy. — [Game Developer, "Five suggestions for 4X fun"](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/five-suggestions-for-4x-fun)

### Inferences
- Meier's gunpowder remark is, in effect, Eden's second half ("the means were at hand") without the first ("someone felt a want"). The classic tree models possibility only; Eden adds motive.
- The designers treat predictability as the fault and clarity as the cost of fixing it. Johnson's test is the one to adopt: the player should always be able to see why a thing has not happened yet. For Eden that means every undiscovered technology should be able to answer "what want is missing, or what means."
- The Civ VII admission that under half of players finished Civ VI is the strongest evidence in this research that the last third of a long history game is where designs fail, not the first.

### Gaps
- No source was found in which Johnson uses the word "beelining"; the term is the community's. His GDC talks were not retrieved, only a listing for his 2022 Old World postmortem. — [Game Developer press release](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/press-release/soren-johnson-delivers-my-elephant-in-the-room-an-old-world-postmortem-at-gdc-2022)
- No Soren Johnson statement on why Civ IV added OR prerequisites was found; the OR-gate description is from fan and guide sources.
- No Amplitude designer statement on Era Stars as an anti-snowball device was found; that framing comes from community threads. — [Amplitude forum thread on Era Star Fame yields](https://community.amplitude-studios.com/amplitude-studios/humankind/forums/210-victor-opendev/threads/40064-on-era-star-fame-yields?page=1)
- Shafer's 2013 essay could not be read, so whether it discusses tech beelining is unknown.
- No Meier statement on how the tech tree was invented was found. One historian argues Meier lifted much of early Civilization from the game Empire. — [The Digital Antiquarian](https://www.filfre.net/?p=3307) (opinionated; treat with care).
- Paradox's stated reason for adding technology spread to Victoria 3 was not retrieved.

## 4. Criticisms, and which fixes are credited with working

### Takeaway
Scholars' complaints are about meaning (one inevitable Western road for everyone); players' complaints are about play (one best order, a tedious last third, a checklist). The fixes with the best reception change what is offered each game. The fix with the most mixed reception, Civ VI Eurekas, left the list of triggers identical every game.

### Cited Findings
- **Determinism and teleology.** Tech trees "reinforce the idea that technological progress is linear, deterministic, and teleological." — [ResearchGate, "Teaching with Technology Trees"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/375672860_Teaching_with_Technology_Trees_-_Game-based_Learning_for_History_Education). A history-education study describes Civilization V as taking "a teleological approach to history that has fallen out of favor among professional historians." — [Society for History Education, King](https://societyforhistoryeducation.org/pdfs/M21_King.pdf)
- **Eurocentrism.** From a postcolonial view, strategy games with a tech tree are held to reinforce a Eurocentric narrative of world history; in Civilization V the eras from ancient to information fix that narrative for every civilization. — [Game Studies 16(2), Ford, "eXplore, eXpand, eXploit, eXterminate"](https://gamestudies.org/1602/articles/ford)
- **Concrete oddities.** In Civilization V, chivalry is a required step toward particle physics, and gunpowder is placed in the Renaissance Era because that is when it reached Europe, not when China made it. — [Game Studies 16(2), Ford](https://gamestudies.org/1602/articles/ford); [Society for History Education, King](https://societyforhistoryeducation.org/pdfs/M21_King.pdf) (the search summary did not separate which source gives which example).
- **Uneven real development.** The tree "levels out and oversimplifies the way technological change works" and presents "a universal trajectory of development from 'less' to 'more' advanced," when real societies achieved advances in some areas while lacking things common elsewhere. — [Sam Badger, "No, 'tech trees' aren't real"](https://sambadger.substack.com/p/no-tech-trees-arent-real)
- **Divergence.** Delays and local hurdles multiplied over many trajectories mean technological history "diverges instead of converging," though there is "undoubtedly a directionality and causality in the tech tree." — [LessWrong, "A humanist critique of technological determinism"](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/wC6nmFKM4EQbQpAhq/a-humanist-critique-of-technological-determinism)
- **Counter-view.** Some scholars reply that this criticism "reduces the games to a homogenous linear structure," and one writes that the concern "is not that the tech tree in Civilization or another game needs to be more accurate"; the objection is to the model of progress. Newer games are credited with adding contingency, Beyond Earth's web among them. — [University of Liverpool thesis, "Playing Games with Technology"](https://livrepository.liverpool.ac.uk/3027693/1/Playing%20Games%20with%20Technology.pdf); [Play the Past](https://www.playthepast.org/?p=4053)
- **"Game objects, not history."** Most tech trees in existence are "game objects, not rigorous and certainly not comprehensive"; a historical tech tree project instead links 1,889 innovations by prerequisite, improvement and inspiration. — [Asterisk, "The Universal Tech Tree"](https://asteriskmag.com/issues/10/the-universal-tech-tree); [Open Culture](https://www.openculture.com/2025/06/a-visualization-of-the-history-of-technology-1889-innovations-across-three-million-years.html)
- **One best path.** Beyond Earth's designers and Soren Johnson both name solved critical paths as the reason to change the structure (see section 3). — [GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/civilization-beyond-earths-space-setting-let-designers-think-outside-the-hex-interview/); [BoardGameGeek quoting Johnson](https://boardgamegeek.com/thread/3659774/design-note-technology-as-contested-territory)
- **Eurekas became a checklist.** Players report "rote tasks the player must do EVERY game, like killing a unit with a slinger or discovering another continent," and getting "the same Eurekas in each game, in more or less the same order." Proposed fix from players: three or four possible boosts per technology with two showing in any one game. One poster argues the root error was the goal that "every tech/civic must have a eureka." Defenders say the boosts add interesting decisions and reward playing the map. — [CivFanatics, "Are Eurekas making every game the same?"](https://forums.civfanatics.com/threads/are-eurekas-making-every-game-the-same.600804/); [CivFanatics, "Eureka system: anyone else started to dislike it?"](https://forums.civfanatics.com/threads/eureka-system-anyone-else-started-to-dislike-it.625210/page-2); [CivFanatics, "Do people actually like Eurekas/Inspirations?"](https://forums.civfanatics.com/threads/do-people-actually-like-eurekas-inspirations.632904/post-15139213) (forum sentiment, not a survey).
- **Random cards split opinion.** Reactions to the Stellaris diary ran from "one of my favourite new design approaches" to "too simplistic for me and too random," with the complaint that chance can block a role-play goal. — [Stellaris Dev Diary 11 thread](https://forum.paradoxplaza.com/forum/developer-diary/stellaris-dev-diary-11-research-technology.893377/)
- **Random subsets split opinion.** Players of Sword of the Stars and similar games praise replayability and the loss of an optimal path, while others dislike losing favourite techs and ask for a switch to turn it off. — [Amplitude forum, tech tree randomised design](https://community.amplitude-studios.com/amplitude-studios/endless-space/forums/28-game-design/threads/12696-tech-tree-randomized-design?page=2) (forum).
- **Old World's deck is well received by its audience.** Commenters on Johnson's notes said the bonus cards create tough choices. — [CivFanatics thread](https://forums.civfanatics.com/threads/soren-johnsons-designer-notes-about-the-tech-tree.672513)
- **Late game drags.** See the Civ VI completion figure in section 3. Civ VII's ages were reported by Beach to raise completion in playtests, but reception was mixed: one review says the decisions "snowball into something far too big and unwieldy to steer," and forum posters called the crises poorly balanced. — [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/firaxis-big-swing-with-civilization-vii-convincing-players-to-actually-finish-their-games); [Slant review](https://www.slantmagazine.com/games/sid-meiers-civilization-vii-review/)
- **Multiple victory routes are not a free fix.** Mitigating the endgame through several victory paths "exacerbates the already fiendish challenge of balancing these complex games as not all victory types are created equal." — [PCGamesN, strategy game endgame design](https://pcgamesn.com/civilization-vi/strategy-game-endgame-design)

### Inferences
- Eden's cause rule answers the scholars' main complaint by construction: a people that never feels cold never gets the cold technologies, so two peoples on different ground will not hold the same things in the same order. That is the uneven development Badger says trees erase.
- The same rule is exposed to the Eureka failure. If "flood plus clay at hand" always produces the same thing on every map, the god player will learn a recipe list. The player-suggested remedy translates directly: give each technology more than one sufficient cause, and let the land decide which is reachable.
- Eden's single goal (first to space) is itself teleological. The criticism bites less if peoples can arrive by different routes and some never arrive.
- Evidence on what "works" is mostly forum sentiment plus one designer's unpublished playtest claim. No controlled or telemetry-based comparison of tech systems was found.

### Gaps
- No source was found on "players ignoring flavour" (not reading technology text or quotes).
- No critic's or reviewer's formal assessment of Eurekas was found, only forum threads.
- The FDG 2015 paper "Can't See the Science for the Trees: Representations of Science in Videogames" surfaced but its content was not retrieved. — [FDG 2015 paper](http://www.fdg2015.org/papers/fdg2015_paper_48.pdf)
- Adam Chapman's "Digital Games as History" was not found by any search run here.

## 5. Space race victory design

### Takeaway
The space race is the best-known built-in climax in strategy games, but the commonly cited weakness is that it turns into a production queue the rivals cannot see or contest. The designs praised for tension stage the finish in visible steps and give rivals a clear warning and a way to respond.

### Cited Findings
- The space race in the Civilization series is called probably the best-known example of a strategy game built toward a climactic ending; the colony ship needs near-future technology and a powerful industrial base. — [Matchstick Eyes, "Strategy games that built to climactic endings"](https://www.matchstickeyes.com/2010/09/22/strategy-games-that-built-to-climactic-endings)
- **Civ VI staging.** Base game and Rise and Fall: launch a satellite, land on the Moon, found a Mars colony. Gathering Storm: satellite, Moon landing, Mars colony, then launch an exoplanet expedition that must travel 50 light-years; repeatable laser-station projects, buildable in parallel at several spaceports, each add one to its speed. Every project needs a city with a Spaceport district. — [Civilization wiki, Victory (Civ6)](https://civilization.fandom.com/wiki/Victory_(Civ6)); [Epic Games, guide to a science victory](https://store.epicgames.com/news/civilization-6-guide-to-a-science-victory)
- **Civ VI weakness: rivals' progress is quiet.** Unlike Civilization V, Civ VI "will not produce a notification of when other players have achieved major milestones towards a certain victory condition." — [Civilization wiki, Victory (Civ6)](https://civilization.fandom.com/wiki/Victory_(Civ6))
- **Civ VI weakness: it reads as a production victory.** Players describe the stage as "mostly a production victory," forced through one city per project in sequence, with every rival sending spies to disrupt rocketry and a counter-spy at the spaceport promoted "every other turn." — [CivFanatics, "Tedious science victory"](https://forums.civfanatics.com/threads/tedious-science-victory.647171) (forum).
- **Beyond Earth: warning and response.** The timeline is similar across victory paths and gives opponents "clear warning" that a player is nearing the end, allowing last-ditch efforts to race for another victory or topple the leader. — [Destructoid review](https://destructoid.com/reviews/review-sid-meiers-civilization-beyond-earth)
- **Civ VII: the victory lap problem.** The final projects feel like a last hurrah but "take too long to build," and finishing the space race "just ended the game with no warning." — [GamerMatters column](https://gamermatters.com/civilization-vii-please-one-more-turn). A review contrasts older games' cutscene for completing the science path with a missing or underwhelming payoff in the newer one. — [Aftermath review](https://aftermath.site/civilization-vii-review/)
- **Player view of realism.** A long-running complaint is that the space race is a "no-brainer" default ending, and that in real history only two powers were really in it. — [CivFanatics, "Ideas for a new space race victory"](https://forums.civfanatics.com/threads/ideas-for-a-new-space-race-victory.142395) (forum).

### Inferences
- Three properties recur in the praised cases and are absent in the criticised ones: the finish is several named steps, each step is announced to everyone, and a rival who sees a step can still do something about it.
- In Eden the player does not build the rocket, so the "production queue" complaint does not apply in its usual form. The risk moves: if the last stretch is only waiting for the people to finish, the god has nothing to do. The Beyond Earth pattern suggests the last stretch should be where interventions matter most (supplying a missing means, or causing a want in a rival people).
- Showing a rival's progress as the same named steps the player's own people are climbing is the cheapest way to make the race felt; Civ VI's removal of milestone notices is cited as a loss.
- A payoff scene at the end is noticed when missing.

### Gaps
- No designer statement was found explaining why any Civilization game staged its space victory the way it did.
- No source was found on Civilization II or IV's spaceship screen, Alpha Centauri's Ascent to Transcendence, or Civilization V's part-by-part assembly and notifications. From memory, unverified: Civ II to V let all players see each rival's ship being assembled part by part, and Civ II and IV allowed capturing the capital to stop a launched ship.
- No official confirmation of how spy sabotage interacts with spaceport projects in Civ VI was found; the claim rests on forum accounts.
- Nothing was found on how non-Civilization games (for example Kerbal-like or tycoon games) pace a space finish.

## 6. Pacing: compressing long early ages, techs per era, playthrough length

### Takeaway
Civilization compresses time by changing how many years a turn stands for, about 40 years at the start and one year at the end, so each era takes a similar share of play regardless of its length in history. Published counts put major historical trees at roughly 70 to 175 technologies. No designer rule for "technologies per era" was found.

### Cited Findings
- **Variable calendar.** A new Civilization game starts in 4000 BCE and each early turn advances 40 years; by the Classical Era a turn is 25 years; turns keep shortening until, in the modern world, every turn is one year. — [Shamus Young, "Civilization Part 2: Games that Stand the Test of Time"](https://www.shamusyoung.com/twentysidedtale/?p=50051)
- **Design aim behind it.** "Ideally, we'd want each of these eras to last roughly the same amount of time so they feel like distinct phases of the game." — [Shamus Young](https://www.shamusyoung.com/twentysidedtale/?p=50051) (a critic's analysis, not a Firaxis statement).
- **Hard stop.** A Standard-speed Civilization VI game is 500 turns or ends in 2050 AD. — [GamePressure, Civ VI game length](https://www.gamepressure.com/sidmeierscivilization6/game-length-how-long-to-complete/z6f7f3)
- **Typical length in turns.** Guides put a standard game at 300 to 500 turns, with most players finishing between 250 and 350. — [Cyberpost, average Civ 6 game turns](https://cyberpost.co/how-many-turns-is-the-average-civ-6-game/) (low-reliability guide site).
- **Typical length in hours.** Estimates run 15 to 40 hours for a standard game; a Game Developer piece cites 15 to 20 hours for a single game; multiplayer posters report 5 to 10 hours at one to two minutes a turn. — [GamePressure](https://www.gamepressure.com/sidmeierscivilization6/game-length-how-long-to-complete/z6f7f3); [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/firaxis-big-swing-with-civilization-vii-convincing-players-to-actually-finish-their-games); [Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/289070/discussions/0/1642052612850867563)
- **Length is the cited reason players quit.** Fewer than 50% completed a Civilization VI game "due to its drawn-out pacing." — [Digital Trends](https://digitaltrends.com/gaming/firaxis-games-civilization-7-finish-games)
- **Chapters as a pacing fix.** Civilization VII breaks the game into ages. — [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/firaxis-big-swing-with-civilization-vii-convincing-players-to-actually-finish-their-games). Humankind resets Era Stars at each era and pays less Fame for slow stars, which gives each era its own short race. — [PC Gamer](https://pcgamer.com/humankind-fame-era-stars). A player describes Era Stars as providing "clear medium goal loops, which are very lacking in many modern 4x strategies." — [Amplitude forum](https://community.amplitude-studios.com/amplitude-studios/humankind/forums/210-victor-opendev/threads/40064-on-era-star-fame-yields?page=1)
- **Counts found.** Civilization VI's technology tree "includes 68 options that span up to seven branches" (launch-era description). — [Fandom, "Breaking Down the Civilization VI Tech Tree"](https://www.fandom.com/articles/breaking-civilization-vi-tech-tree) (the search summary did not confirm whether this or the [GameRevolution tree guide](https://www.gamerevolution.com/guides/71105-sid-meiers-civilization-vi-full-technology-tree) carried the figure).
- **Counts found.** Victoria 3 planned about 175 technologies across three trees for a 100-year game, with leaders discovering about one per year. — [Victoria 3 Dev Diary 27](https://admin-forum.paradoxplaza.com/forum/developer-diary/victoria-3-dev-diary-27-technology.1502428)
- **Counts found.** EU4 has seven institutions appearing across its span. — [EU4 Development Diary, 26 May 2016](https://forum.paradoxplaza.com/forum/threads/eu4-development-diary-26th-of-may-2016.937696/). CK3 has four eras of innovations. — [CK3 Dev Diary 27](https://forum.paradoxplaza.com/forum/threads/ckiii-dev-diary-27-cultures-cultural-innovations.1391886/)
- **Pace complaints run both ways.** Civ VI players complained of racing through eras too fast; Dawn of Man players complained that knowledge points "arrive in a slow trickle, even when playing on the fastest game speed." — [CivFanatics, "is it speeding through the eras?"](https://forums.civfanatics.com/threads/the-state-of-the-game-is-it-speeding-through-the-eras.642254/page-2); [Steam discussion, Dawn of Man](https://steamcommunity.com/app/858810/discussions/0/3581993464535559632)

### Inferences
- Arithmetic on the cited figures: 68 technologies over a typical 250 to 350 turn finish is one new technology about every 4 to 5 turns; at 15 to 20 hours that is roughly one every 13 to 18 minutes of play. Victoria 3's leaders get one per game-year. These are the only rate figures the evidence supports.
- The variable calendar is a planned form, not a cause. For Eden, which may not gate on timers, the equivalent is to let simulated years run fast when nothing is changing and slow when much is, and to state that as a display rule about time, not a rule about when things may happen. If adopted it would need listing under "Planned on purpose."
- Observed lane counts, for the "how many branches" question: Civ VI up to seven rows wide, Humankind seven star categories, Victoria 3 three trees, CK3 three categories by four eras, Civ VI two parallel trees. Nothing found argues for a number; the surveyed range is three to seven lanes.
- The Humankind and Civ VII pattern (a short race inside each era, then a reset) is the industry's current answer to 10,000-year pacing. Eden could get the same beat from a recurring "first people to..." marker per age without gating anything on it.

### Gaps
- **The counts in the brief are unverified.** No search result confirmed "Civ VI about 77 techs plus 61 civics" or "Humankind about 90." From memory, unverified: 68 technologies and about 50 civics is the Civ VI base game, and 77 and 61 are the totals after the Gathering Storm expansion. A Civilization wiki comparison page exists but its numbers were not in the snippet. — [Civilization wiki, Comparison between Civilization games/Technologies](https://civilization.fandom.com/wiki/Comparison_between_Civilization_games/Technologies)
- No technology counts were found for Civilization I to V, Humankind, Old World or Stellaris. One unattributed figure of "86 different technologies" appeared on a franchise page without naming the game. — [Giant Bomb, Civilization franchise](https://giantbomb.com/wiki/Franchises/Civilization)
- No designer statement on the right number of technologies per era was found.
- No playthrough length was found for Humankind or Old World.
- The exact year-per-turn schedule per era from an official source was not found.

## 7. Published analyses of tech tree graphs

### Takeaway
Academic work on tech trees as graphs is thin and comes mostly from one researcher, Teemu Heinimäki, who noted in 2012 that scholarly interest was "almost nonexistent." No published table of node counts, depth, branching factor or number of independent paths across major games was found.

### Cited Findings
- Heinimäki's 2012 paper states that technology trees had been used in the digital game industry for over two decades but "the academic interest towards the matter seems to be almost nonexistent." — [ACM, "Technology Trees in Digital Gaming," MindTrek 2012](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1145/2393132.2393139)
- His 2015 doctoral thesis proposes a generic way to implement tech trees and a prototype, the Tech Tree Tool, with "TT measuring (and limited automatic adjusting) capabilities." — [Tampere University, "Technology Trees and Tools: Constructing Development Graphs for Digital Games"](https://trepo.tuni.fi/handle/10024/113957)
- A related 2015 journal article with Tapio Elomaa, "Quality Measures for Improving Technology Trees," argues that tech tree quality "can be improved by adjusting their structural and quantitative properties." — [Tampere University research portal](https://researchportal.tuni.fi/en/publications/technology-trees-and-tools-constructing-development-graphs-for-di/)
- Ghys's paper is the only cross-game numerical comparison found (the overlap percentages in section 2). — [Game Studies 12(1), Ghys](https://www.gamestudies.org/1201/articles/tuur_ghys)
- A machine-readable graph of the Civilization VI tree exists as a community dataset. — [GitHub, LKchemposer/civ6-graph](https://www.github.com/LKchemposer/civ6-graph) (existence only; contents not examined).
- A fan-written structural study of the Civilization IV tree exists. — [CivFanatics, "Tech Tree Analysis (first instalment)"](https://forums.civfanatics.com/threads/tech-tree-analysis-first-instalment.157540/) (existence only; contents not retrieved).
- Outside games, one project maps 1,889 real innovations across three million years with typed links. — [Open Culture](https://www.openculture.com/2025/06/a-visualization-of-the-history-of-technology-1889-innovations-across-three-million-years.html)

### Inferences
- Since no reference numbers exist, the designer can measure Eden's own graph and compare it against the two public datasets above. The useful measures, by the shape of the criticism in sections 3 and 4, are: longest chain to the goal, number of technologies not on any chain to the goal, and how many technologies have more than one sufficient cause.
- Heinimäki's quality measures are the only published attempt at "what makes a tree good" in numbers and are worth reading in full before settling Eden's tree.

### Gaps
- Which quality measures Heinimäki defines, and any values he reports for real games, were not retrieved.
- No source classifying AND versus OR prerequisite structures formally was found.
- No count of truly independent paths for any major game was found.

## 8. Games where technology is discovered through the simulation

### Takeaway
Paradox has gone furthest: in its games technology arrives because of where a country sits, who its neighbours are and how literate its people are. Players accept these systems as more believable and as a brake on runaway leaders, and complain when the outcome feels like luck or geography they cannot affect.

### Cited Findings
- **Europa Universalis IV institutions (2016).** Seven institutions appear over the game. If an institution does not spread into a country and get embraced by its government, that country's technology costs slowly rise. Technology group no longer affects research, and Westernization was removed; modernisation happens in steps as institutions spread across borders. — [EU4 Development Diary, 26 May 2016](https://forum.paradoxplaza.com/forum/threads/eu4-development-diary-26th-of-may-2016.937696/)
- **EU4 revision (2021, patch 1.32).** Each technology has a fixed set of required institutions; each one not yet embraced makes that technology cost 50% more. — [EU4 Development Diary, 28 September 2021](https://forum.paradoxplaza.com/forum/threads/europa-universalis-iv-development-diary-28th-september-2021.1492744)
- **EU4 reception.** Some praised the design as compelling and the penalties as more dynamic than the old single leap; others objected that it could be "extremely random" and give players far from Europe little benefit. — [EU4 Development Diary thread, 26 May 2016](https://forum.paradoxplaza.com/forum/threads/eu4-development-diary-26th-of-may-2016.937696/) (forum).
- **Victoria 3 innovation and spread.** Innovation comes from universities and is capped by literacy. A country always has one technology from each of the three trees being passively researched through spread; spread only works on technologies some other country already has; innovation beyond the literacy cap, or with no project chosen, flows into spread. Modifiers include free-speech law (Protected Speech +25%), isolationism, great-power status and literacy. — [Victoria 3 Dev Diary 27](https://admin-forum.paradoxplaza.com/forum/developer-diary/victoria-3-dev-diary-27-technology.1502428); [GamePressure, Victoria 3 technologies](https://www.gamepressure.com/victoria-3/technologies/z410577); [Gameplay.tips, innovation and tech penalties](https://gameplay.tips/guides/victoria-3-innovation-and-tech-penalties-guide.html); [TheGamer](https://www.thegamer.com/victoria-3-technology-tree-eras-best-spread/) (the guides are from launch in 2022 and do not fully agree on how literacy and surplus interact).
- **Crusader Kings III innovations.** Technology belongs to a culture, not a realm. Two processes run at once, each on one innovation at a time. Exposure is passive: when the culture's counties border a culture that already has an innovation, the game picks a suitable one at random to advance. Fascination is chosen by the cultural head, whose Learning skill sets its rate. — [CK3 Dev Diary 27](https://forum.paradoxplaza.com/forum/threads/ckiii-dev-diary-27-cultures-cultural-innovations.1391886/)
- **CK3 numbers (fan guide, may be out of date).** Chance to advance (the guide's summary did not state the interval): base 5%, plus 40% if exposure applies, plus the fascination bonus of 20% plus 2% per point of the head's Learning. — [GameWatcher](https://gamewatcher.com/news/crusader-kings-3-culture-and-innovations)
- **Civilization VI Eurekas.** Nearly every technology has an associated in-world activity; doing it grants a boost toward that technology, so research speed is not purely science output. — [TechBloat, quoting Beach](https://www.techbloat.com/how-civilization-vis-turns-research-and-victory-conditions-work.html); [Gamasutra/Game Developer](https://gamasutra.com/design/-i-civilization-vi-i-s-lead-designer-wants-to-shake-up-players). Reception: liked for tying research to the map; disliked for being the same list every game (section 4). — [CivFanatics](https://forums.civfanatics.com/threads/are-eurekas-making-every-game-the-same.600804/)
- **Dawn of Man milestones.** Knowledge points are earned by what the settlement actually does and sees, then spent by the player; technologies can also be bought from traders with surplus goods. — [Twinfinite](https://twinfinite.net/guides/dawn-of-man-walkthrough/); [TechRaptor review](https://techraptor.net/content/dawn-of-man-review/). Reception: the milestone link is liked, but it is gamed (players build surplus buildings in sets of five for the points, then recycle them; others mass-produce bows to buy technology) and the trickle is called slow. — [TechRaptor tips](https://techraptor.net/gaming/guides/dawn-of-man-tips-tricks-for-new-players-crops-efficiency-raiders-and-more); [Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/858810/discussions/0/3581993464535559632)
- **Stellaris situational cards.** Some technologies cannot be drawn in the normal deck and only appear after in-world events such as researching anomalies or studying debris. — [Stellaris Dev Diary 11](https://forum.paradoxplaza.com/forum/developer-diary/stellaris-dev-diary-11-research-technology.893377/); [Stellaris Dev Diary 25, reverse engineering](https://admin-forum.paradoxplaza.com/forum/developer-diary/stellaris-dev-diary-25-reverse-engineering-and-unique-technologies.913539/page-2)
- **Anno 1800.** New buildings and production chains unlock because a class of residents exists whose needs have been met; the developers link the worker tier to "the demand from factories for specialized workers." — [Anno Union devblog](https://www.anno-union.com/en/devblog-residential-tiers/)

### Inferences
- Paradox's diffusion systems have three working parts, and Eden can take all three without breaking the cause rule: a thing can only spread if someone already has it (Victoria 3); it spreads across a shared border or contact (CK3 exposure, EU4); and the receiving people need the capacity to take it up (Victoria 3 literacy, EU4 embracing). Contact is a cause, so diffusion counts as "means at hand."
- Dawn of Man shows what happens when simulation events feed a counter: players manufacture the events. Building five huts to recycle them is the exploit. Eden's wants are harder to farm because a want is felt, not counted, but the god player can cause wants, so the same incentive exists. A want deliberately caused should cost the people something real.
- The "feels random" complaint against EU4 institutions and Stellaris cards is the mirror of the "same every game" complaint against Eurekas. The accepted middle, in Johnson's terms, is that the player can always see why. A readable reason for each advance and each absence is the feature these receptions most reward.
- CK3's split, one passive process and one chosen process, is a direct model for a god game: the world advances one thing by itself, and the god nudges one other.

### Gaps
- **Dwarf Fortress.** No source was found for the claim that it has no technology progression or for a stated cut-off year. From memory, unverified: Tarn Adams has said the game deliberately excludes technology later than about 1400. Searches of his interviews returned nothing on this.
- No Madruga Works developer statement on Dawn of Man's design intent was found; all detail is from guides, reviews and player threads.
- How Victoria 3 picks which technology a country receives through spread was not found.
- No reception data beyond forum sentiment was found for any of these systems.
- CK3 and Victoria 3 values come from launch-era guides and may have changed in later patches.
