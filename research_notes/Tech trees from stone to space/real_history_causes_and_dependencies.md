## 1. The advance-by-advance account: need, prerequisites, resource, date and place, what it enabled

### Takeaway
Across about forty advances, the pattern is consistent: a standing want (food, warmth, light, cheaper labour, winning a war, keeping accounts) is nearly always present for a long time, and the advance appears only when the last missing prerequisite or local resource arrives. Crises decide *where and when* a thing is taken up; prior techniques decide *whether it can exist at all*.

### Cited Findings

Each record: **Need** (what historians credit) / **Needs first** (prerequisites) / **Ground** (resource or geography) / **When, where** / **Opens** (what it made possible) / **Pull or push** (demand-led or supply-led, my classification).

#### A. Foragers (before farming)

- **Control of fire.**
  - When, where: claims range back to about 2.0 Ma; charred bones deep inside Wonderwerk Cave, South Africa, come from a layer dated between 1.07 and 1.79 Ma — [Wikipedia: Control of fire by early humans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Control_of_fire_by_early_humans); [IFLScience on Wonderwerk](https://iflscience.com/earliest-evidence-for-fire-use-suggests-homo-erectus-may-have-transported-flames-179-million-years-ago-83786). The best-established early hearth-like evidence is Gesher Benot Ya'aqov, Israel, about 790 ka — [Goren-Inbar et al., Science 2004](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004Sci...304..725G/abstract).
  - Debated: at Gesher only about 2% of flint and wood fragments are burnt, which the excavators use to rule out wildfire, but critics note no site of that age has a built hearth — [Science News](https://sciencenews.org/?p=36578); [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/scorched-remains-suggest/).
  - Need: warmth, cooking, protection from predators, light **[unverified]**.
  - Needs first: nothing but fuel and the habit of tending; fire was probably kept and carried long before it could be made **[inference]** from the "transported flames" reading of Wonderwerk — [IFLScience](https://iflscience.com/earliest-evidence-for-fire-use-suggests-homo-erectus-may-have-transported-flames-179-million-years-ago-83786).
  - Opens: cooking, pottery firing, lime, metal smelting, glass — every later heat process **[inference]**.
  - Pull or push: pull (constant need), gated by skill **[inference]**.

- **Stone tools.**
  - When, where: Lomekwi 3, Kenya, 3.3 Ma, about 700,000 years older than the Gona tools of Ethiopia (2.6 Ma) and older than the earliest *Homo* fossils; some researchers remain sceptical of their use — [Wikipedia: Lomekwi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lomekwi); [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/ancient-stone-tools-force-rethinking-of-human-origins/).
  - Ground: flakeable stone (flint, chert, obsidian, basalt) **[unverified]**.
  - Opens: butchery, woodworking, hide working, and later hafting **[inference]**.

- **Hafting (stone point on a shaft).**
  - When, where: Kathu Pan 1, South Africa, about 500 ka; this pushed stone-tipped spears back about 200,000 years from the previous earliest evidence — [Wilkins et al., Science 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23161998/); [University of Toronto](https://utoronto.ca/news/archaeologists-identify-spear-tips-used-hunting-half-million-years-ago).
  - Needs first: stone points, wooden shafts, binding (sinew, plant fibre, resin) **[unverified]**. It is the first *combination* technology in the record **[inference]**, which fits Arthur's thesis below.
  - Opens: spears, axes, adzes, and later the hoe and sickle **[inference]**.

- **Clothing.**
  - When: the body louse split from the head louse at least 83 ka and possibly 170 ka (95% interval 29–691 ka); an earlier study gave 107 ka. The body louse lives only in clothing, so clothing must exist by then — [Toups et al., Mol. Biol. Evol. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3002236).
  - Need: cold **[unverified: the paper's own argument about why clothing arose was not retrieved]**.
  - Needs first: hide scraping tools, awls, later needles **[unverified]**.
  - Pull or push: pull (cold) **[inference]**.

- **Pottery.**
  - When, where: Xianrendong Cave, Jiangxi, China, 20–19 ka, made by hunter-gatherers thousands of years before farming; Yuchanyan Cave, Hunan, about 18.3 ka — [Wu et al., Science 2012](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012Sci...336.1696W/abstract); [review article](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/epdfplus/10.1086/659965).
  - Need: cooking (soot and scorch marks); other groups used pots for storage and brewing — [Boston University](https://www.bu.edu/articles/2012/a-prehistoric-pottery-barn/).
  - Important for a tree: pottery does **not** depend on farming or villages. It came first in East Asia and after farming in the Near East **[inference]** from the dates above and the farming dates below.
  - Ground: clay, fuel **[unverified]**. Opens: boiling, storage of liquids, brewing, and the kiln knowledge later reused for smelting **[inference]**.

#### B. Farming villages

- **Storage and granaries (before domesticated crops).**
  - When, where: Dhra', Jordan, 11,300–11,175 cal BP; round mud-walled stores about 3 m across with raised floors on notched stones to keep out rodents and damp; they held wild barley and pre-date domesticated crops and large villages by roughly a millennium — [Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/ancient-granaries-preceded-agricultural-revolution); [ScienceDaily (Kuijt and Finlayson, PNAS 2009)](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2009/06/090623150619.htm).
  - The earlier Natufians had seasonal settlements but little evidence of storage — [Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/ancient-granaries-preceded-agricultural-revolution).
  - Order shown by the evidence: gather wild grain, then store it, then settle, then domesticate. "Fundamental social changes occurred before plant domestication" (Kuijt) — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2009/06/090623150619.htm).

- **Plant domestication (agriculture).**
  - When, where: at least 11 independent regions; Fertile Crescent pre-domestication cultivation about 11,000 BP; Yellow and Yangtze rivers about 9,000 BP; Mesoamerica about 8,000 BP; New Guinea highlands 10,000–6,500 BP — [World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/article/1886/origins-of-world-agriculture/); [Bellwood, ANU Press](https://press-files.anu.edu.au/downloads/press/n2540/html/ch02.xhtml).
  - It was slow: cultivation of wild cereals lasted perhaps 1,000–2,000 years before domesticated forms appear — [Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/ancient-granaries-preceded-agricultural-revolution).
  - Need: debated. Population pressure (Boserup's direction of cause, see section 2), climate change at the end of the Ice Age, and social competition have all been proposed **[unverified for the named climate and feasting theories]**.
  - Ground: wild plants that can be domesticated; Diamond's argument is that these were very unevenly spread — [Wikipedia: Guns, Germs, and Steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guns,_Germs,_and_Steel).
  - Opens: surplus, population density, specialists, everything after **[inference]**.

- **Animal domestication.**
  - Diamond counts 14 large (over 45 kg) domesticated mammals: 13 from Eurasia, one from South America, none elsewhere — [Wikipedia: Guns, Germs, and Steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guns,_Germs,_and_Steel).
  - Opens: traction (plough, cart), wool, milk, manure, pack transport **[inference]**; see plough below.

- **Irrigation.**
  - When, where: canals at Choga Mami, eastern Iraq, about 6000 BC (one source says about 5500 BC); a 2025 study mapped more than 200 large canals near Eridu, in use from the sixth millennium BC to the early first millennium BC — [Wikipedia: Choga Mami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choga_Mami); [Guinness World Records](https://guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/first-irrigation-canals); [Durham University](https://dur.ac.uk/news-events/latest-news/2025/03/archaeologists-discover-ancient-irrigation-network-in-mesopotamia--/).
  - Ground: a river beside dry, flat, fertile land. Mesopotamian floods were less predictable than the Nile's, which made planning harder **[unverified: stated in a search summary without a clear source page]**.
  - Pull or push: pull; this is Boserup's intensification step, taken when more food is needed from the same land **[inference]** from [EH.net review of Boserup](https://eh.net/book_reviews/the-conditions-of-agricultural-growth-the-economics-of-agrarian-change-under-population-pressure/).

- **The plough (ard).**
  - When, where: evidence for the ard in the Near East in the 6th millennium BC; the oldest dated plough marks are from the first half of the 4th millennium BC in Denmark and northern Germany — [Wikipedia: Ard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ard_(plough)).
  - Needs first: domesticated oxen ("enabled the true ard") — [Wikipedia: Plough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plough).
  - Social effect: farmers rich enough to keep specialised plough oxen farmed extra land, so the plough widened inequality — [Nature HSS Communications 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-024-02837-5).
  - Boserup's point applies: the plough costs more labour overall (feeding animals) and is only worth adopting once fallows are short and land is scarce — [EH.net review](https://eh.net/book_reviews/the-conditions-of-agricultural-growth-the-economics-of-agrarian-change-under-population-pressure/).

- **The wheel.**
  - When: about 3500 BC — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/why-it-took-so-long-to-inv/).
  - Needs first: fine carpentry for a round wheel on a fitted axle, which needed cast copper chisels and gouges, common in the Near East only after about 4000 BC (David Anthony) — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/why-it-took-so-long-to-inv/). So **copper tools gate the wheel**.
  - Debated origin: Anthony thinks it was invented once and spread too fast to locate; a 2024 Royal Society Open Science study proposes Carpathian copper miners and gradual evolution from rollers, which Anthony rejects — [Georgia Tech](https://ae.gatech.edu/news/2024/11/engineering-origin-wheel); [Discover](https://www.discovermagazine.com/archaeologists-have-long-sought-but-never-found-the-very-first-wheel-41299).
  - Ground: draught animals and fairly flat, open country. Mesoamericans made wheeled figurines but no carts — [JSTOR Daily](https://daily.jstor.org/how-was-the-wheel-invented). They had no large draught animal (Diamond's count above), which is the usual explanation **[inference]**.

- **Weaving.** No source found this session; see Gaps.

#### C. Metals, cities, records

- **Copper smelting.**
  - When, where: Belovode, eastern Serbia (Vinča culture), about 5000 BC, the earliest securely dated smelting anywhere; ore came from nearby deposits such as Rudna Glava — [Radivojević, The Rise of Metallurgy in Eurasia](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10134145/7/Radivojevic_Ch11_The%20Rise%20of%20Metallurgy%20in%20Eurasia.pdf); [UCL project page](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/social-historical-sciences/archaeology/research/research-directory/material-worlds/rise-metallurgy-eurasia).
  - The find revived the case for several origins of metallurgy in Eurasia instead of one — [Radivojević et al.](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1470180/1/S0959774314001097a.pdf).
  - Needs first: high-temperature kilns (pottery), charcoal, knowledge of coloured ores (first used as pigments) **[unverified]**.

- **Bronze.**
  - First bronzes were arsenical copper, because arsenic occurs with copper ore; tin bronze replaced it. Britain completed the switch about 2200–2100 BC — [Wikipedia: Tin sources and trade during antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tin_sources_and_trade_during_antiquity).
  - Ground: tin is rare and "usually had to be traded over very long distances" — [same](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tin_sources_and_trade_during_antiquity). Tin ingots from Bronze Age wrecks off Israel and southern France have been traced to Cornwall and Devon; the trade is estimated at tens of tons a year, perhaps up to 200 — [Antiquity 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/from-lands-end-to-the-levant-did-britains-tin-sources-transform-the-bronze-age-in-europe-and-the-mediterranean/2330F3B6498B210DA61B89026A1F38EA); [Durham University](https://dur.ac.uk/news-events/latest-news/2025/05/britains-long-distance-tin-trade-transformed-the-bronze-age/).
  - So bronze **requires long-distance trade** (ships, pack animals, trusted exchange) for almost every society **[inference]**.

- **Iron.**
  - When, where: smelted iron in Anatolia a little after 2000 BC, used sparingly; general adoption 1200–1000 BC — [Erb-Satullo, J. Archaeological Research 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10814-019-09129-6).
  - Why then: one line of argument is a shortage — the collapse of the trade that supplied copper and tin pushed smiths to iron. Current opinion sees iron's spread as a *result* of the Late Bronze Age collapse, not its cause — [Erb-Satullo 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10814-019-09129-6).
  - Ground: iron ore is common almost everywhere, unlike tin **[unverified]**; that is why iron, once mastered, was the cheap metal **[inference]**.

- **Writing (from accounting tokens).**
  - Sequence (Schmandt-Besserat): plain clay tokens from about 7500 BC, each shape one unit of one commodity, used to pool and redistribute community surplus; then tokens sealed in clay envelopes to hold a record of a debt, with the tokens' shapes pressed on the outside; then, about 3200 BC, the envelope became a solid tablet carrying only the impressed signs — [Schmandt-Besserat, UT Austin](https://sites.utexas.edu/dsb/tokens/tokens-and-writing-the-cognitive-development/); [From Accounting to Writing](https://sites.utexas.edu/dsb/tokens/from-accounting-to-writing/); [Utah State course notes](https://www.usu.edu/markdamen/HIST1100/chapters/16TOKENS.htm).
  - Need: record-keeping of stored goods and debts, not literature. "The bureaucrats in both cultures needed accountants before writers or scribes" — [Joseph, The Crest of the Peacock, ch. 4](https://www.ms.uky.edu/~dhje223/CrestOfThePeacockCh4-pages-2-21.pdf).
  - Needs first: stored surplus, an institution that collects it (temple, palace), clay.
  - Caveat: the sources found are Schmandt-Besserat's own pages or teaching notes that endorse her; critics exist but were not retrieved.

- **Mathematics and calendars.**
  - Mesopotamian mathematics arose in the late fourth millennium BC alongside the script, both for accounting; base-60 notation dates to the third millennium BC — [Joseph, ch. 4](https://www.ms.uky.edu/~dhje223/CrestOfThePeacockCh4-pages-2-21.pdf); [UC Riverside notes](https://math.ucr.edu/~res/math153/history01.pdf).
  - Egypt and Ebla used arithmetic and astronomy to record time and build calendars; the regular Nile flood shaped the farming year — [Wikipedia: History of mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_mathematics).
  - Need: administration, tax, land measurement, timing of floods and sowing **[inference]** from the above.

- **Coinage.**
  - When, where: Lydia (western Anatolia), late 7th century BC, electrum (natural gold-silver alloy) stamped with a royal mark; Lydian smiths could already part silver from gold and adjust the alloy — [World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/article/797/the-invention-of-the-first-coinage-in-ancient-lydia/); [LBMA](https://www.lbma.org.uk/wonders-of-gold/items/lydian-electrum-coin).
  - Why: unsettled. Proposals include tax collection, state payments and long-distance trade; it is unclear the first coins circulated in ordinary commerce, and one economist finds all existing explanations wanting — [Velde, "On the Origin of Specie"](https://www.atlantafed.org/-/media/documents/news/conferences/2012/monetary-economics/papers/velde.pdf); [World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/article/797/the-invention-of-the-first-coinage-in-ancient-lydia/).
  - Needs first: metallurgy and assaying, weights and measures, a state whose stamp is trusted **[inference]**.

- **Sailing ships.**
  - Earliest evidence: a painted disc from As-Sabiyah, Kuwait (Ubaid period) showing mast and sail; the long-cited Eridu boat models are now questioned; in Egypt sails appear at the start of Naqada IIc (late fourth millennium BC) — [Antiquity, "Boat models from Eridu"](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/boat-models-from-eridu-sailing-or-spinning-during-the-ubaid-period/B85B9F6577AF4B436178266D94FE4ED9); [Antiquity, "Egypt's earliest sailing ships"](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/egypts-earliest-sailing-ships/28F7218895B44D2DC9BF36AECD5B3F79).
  - Needs first: woven cloth or matting for the sail, rope, plank or reed hulls **[unverified]**. Need: bulk trade (the tin and copper trade above is the clearest case) **[inference]**.

#### D. Classical and medieval machines and materials

- **Water mills and windmills.**
  - Water mill: first technical description by Vitruvius, 40–10 BC (undershot wheel with gearing). Domesday Book (England, 1086) records about 6,000 water mills — [Wikipedia: Watermill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watermill); [Mills Archive](https://new.millsarchive.org/2003/01/01/historical-introduction-to-mills-and-milling/3/).
  - Windmill: vertical-axis mills in eastern Persia (Seistan), recorded in the 9th–10th centuries (Britannica: a reference in AD 644 and mills in Seistan in 915). First clear European reference: Weedley, Yorkshire, 1185 (post mills; Historic England puts the earliest before 1137) — [Britannica](https://britannica.com/technology/windmill); [Guinness](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/first-windmills); [Historic England](https://historicengland.org.uk/hex/file/he/content/upload/10902.pdf).
  - Need: grinding grain, the largest daily labour cost of a farming household **[unverified]**. Ground: a stream with fall, or steady wind where streams are lacking (Seistan, the English lowlands) **[inference]**.
  - Opens: gearing, cams, trip hammers, bellows — the mechanical vocabulary later used by clocks, textile mills and blast furnaces **[inference]**.

- **Masonry, concrete, arches.**
  - Roman concrete: lime plus pozzolana, the volcanic sand of Pozzuoli near Naples; Vitruvius gives 1 lime to 3 pozzolana for buildings and 1 to 2 under water. The Pantheon dome is the largest unreinforced concrete dome. Why it lasts is still debated (seawater reaction versus hot-mixing of quicklime) — [Wikipedia: Roman concrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_concrete); [Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/the-secrets-of-ancient-romes-buildings-234992/).
  - Ground: limestone and fuel for lime everywhere; volcanic ash only in some places **[inference]**.

- **Paper.**
  - When, where: traditionally Cai Lun, Han China, 105 CE, but he probably improved a known process; a paper map fragment from Fangmatan, Gansu, dates to 179–141 BCE. Fabriano, Italy, was making paper by the mid-13th century — [Oxford, Cabinet: Origins of paper](https://www.cabinet.ox.ac.uk/node/7013); [Wikipedia: History of paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_paper); [Gonville and Caius College](https://www.cai.cam.ac.uk/node/6697).
  - Ground: plant fibre and, in Europe, linen rags collected by mills — [Clemson open text](https://opentextbooks.clemson.edu/sciencetechnologyandsociety/chapter/the-first-moveable-type-printing-press/). So European paper depends on a population already wearing linen **[inference]**.

- **Printing with movable metal type (Gutenberg; Mainz, 1450s [place and date unverified this session]).**
  - A textbook combination of earlier techniques: cheap rag paper; an alloy of lead, tin and antimony that melts low and casts sharp; a hand mould for mass-casting identical letters (the key invention); oil-based ink (lamp black in linseed or walnut oil) because water ink would not hold on metal; a screw press adapted from wine, oil or paper presses — [Britannica](https://britannica.com/topic/printing-publishing/The-invention-of-printing); [National Diet Library of Japan](https://ndl.go.jp/incunabula/e/chapter1/chapter1_01.html); [Wikipedia: Printing press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printing_press).
  - Need: demand for books; not covered by the sources found (see Gaps).

- **Gunpowder.** Developed by Chinese alchemists in the Tang dynasty; a Chinese text of about 850 AD is the usual first reference — [Wikipedia: Four Great Inventions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Great_Inventions). A by-product of alchemy, not of a military request: push, then pulled by war **[inference]**. Ground: saltpetre, sulphur, charcoal **[unverified]**.

- **Compass.** Lodestone devices in Han China; first description of a suspended magnetic needle by Shen Kuo, 1088; Song ships used it to reach Middle Eastern markets — [Wikipedia: Four Great Inventions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Great_Inventions); [EBSCO: Song science and technology](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/science-and-technology-song-dynasty). Ground: lodestone (natural magnetite).

- **Mechanical clocks.** Su Song's water-driven astronomical clock tower, China, 1092; a mechanical escapement appears in Europe in the codices of Alfonso the Wise about 1272, and its exact origin "has presented one of the most tantalizing of problems" (Derek de Solla Price) — [EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/science-and-technology-song-dynasty); [Project Gutenberg, Price](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/30001.kf8.images). Mokyr and Kelly and Ó Gráda use the 18th-century British watch trade as the model of steady precision improvement by anonymous craftsmen — [Mokyr 2017](https://sites.northwestern.edu/jmokyr/files/2018/02/Bottom-up-or-top-down-2fp03ny.pdf). Clockmakers are the seed of precision machining **[inference]**.

- **Glass and optics.** Spectacle lenses existed from about 1300 and "became better and better"; grinding both faces developed in the 16th century. Hans Lipperhey, a spectacle maker of Middelburg, applied for a telescope patent on 2 October 1608; it was refused partly because others (Jacob Metius within weeks, later claims for Zacharias Janssen) had the same device and it was easy to copy — [Oxford History of Science Museum](https://www.cabinet.ox.ac.uk/telescope); [Experimental Astronomy 2009](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10686-009-9160-y); [Wikipedia: Hans Lipperhey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Lipperhey).
  - Reading: the telescope was "not some lucky accident" but the outcome of an existing spectacle trade — [World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/article/2270). Near-simultaneous claimants show it was *ripe*: supply-led **[inference]**.
  - Chain: glassmaking, then spectacles (demand: ageing readers), then telescope and microscope, then astronomy and the evidence base of the new science **[inference]**.

- **The scientific method.** Mokyr: early modern Europe developed a "Republic of Letters", a cross-border community that tested and filtered ideas, helped by political fragmentation; Francis Bacon is his key "cultural entrepreneur"; "propositional knowledge" (knowing why) is what kept the Industrial Revolution from fizzling out. Critics say too much weight rests on Bacon — [Econlib on Mokyr](https://www.econlib.org/library/enc/bios/mokyr.html); [review, J. Institutional Economics](https://dx.doi.org/10.1017%2FS1744137421000588). Dartnell calls the scientific method "the greatest invention of them all" for a rebuilding society — [Wikipedia: The Knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Knowledge:_How_to_Rebuild_Our_World_from_Scratch).
  - Needs first: printing and paper (cheap circulation of results), instruments (lenses, clocks, the air pump) **[inference]**.

#### E. Coal, steam, iron, machines

- **Coal as fuel (England, 16th–18th centuries).**
  - Nef's thesis: a "timber crisis" — wood demand for fuel and building pushed prices up and drove England to coal. Challenged by Flinn and Hammersley (Nef used only London prices; woodland was abundant in much of the country). Allen's middle view: no national shortage, but London's huge market raised wood prices enough to give coal roughly a 50% discount there. Steinmueller (2013): timber growing was sustainable and shortage claims may partly reflect coal interests — [Berkeley Economic Review summary](https://econreview.studentorg.berkeley.edu/the-roots-of-modernity-energy-crises-industrialization-and-technological-change/); [Steinmueller, Research Policy 2013](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/respol/v42y2013i10p1739-1748.html).
  - Ground: coal seams near water transport **[inference]**.

- **Steam pump and engine.**
  - Problem: as shallow coal was worked out, pits went deeper and flooded; flooding of coal and tin mines was a major problem — [Britannica: Newcomen](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Thomas-Newcomen); [ASME landmark](https://www.asme.org/wwwasmeorg/media/ResourceFiles/AboutASME/Who%20We%20Are/Engineering%20History/Landmarks/70-Newcomen-Engine.pdf).
  - Savery's 1698 pump lifted water only about 50 feet and could not run continuously — [APS News](https://aps.org/apsnews/2018/07/savery-steam-engine).
  - Newcomen and Calley, 1712, near Dudley Castle, West Midlands: first working piston engine, 12 strokes a minute, about 120 gallons a minute — [Wikipedia: Thomas Newcomen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Newcomen).
  - Key fact for a cause model: the engine was very wasteful of fuel, "but as it operated in coal mines, that was not a problem" — [Wikipedia: Newcomen atmospheric engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newcomen_atmospheric_engine). It was viable only where coal was nearly free.
  - Allen generalises this: the steam engine and spinning jenny "were adopted in Britain because labour was expensive and coal was cheap" — [Allen, Cambridge excerpt](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805218/68273/excerpt/9780521868273_excerpt.pdf).
  - Needs first: the science of the vacuum and atmospheric pressure, cast iron and brass cylinders, pump-making **[unverified]**.

- **Iron with coke; steel at scale.**
  - Abraham Darby, Coalbrookdale, Shropshire, 1709: first marketable iron smelted with coke instead of charcoal, near local coal — [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Abraham-Darby); [EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/invention-coke-smelting). Coalbrookdale then cast cylinders for Newcomen engines from the 1720s and later for Boulton and Watt — [Sorbonne, Ironbridge](https://18thc-cities.sorbonne-universite.fr/Ironbridge.html). Iron and steam fed each other **[inference]**.
  - Bessemer converter, 1856: up to 30 tons of steel in 20–30 minutes; steel fell from about £50 a ton to £4 by 1875 — [World History Encyclopedia (secondary; attribution from a search summary)](https://www.worldhistory.org/audioplayer/en/2-2206/).
  - Ground: coal and iron ore close together, with cheap carriage.

- **Machine tools and precision.**
  - John Wilkinson's cylinder-boring machine (1775; some accounts say 1774) made cylinders true enough for Watt's engine — [Smiles, Industrial Biography](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/404/pg404.html). It came from cannon boring, a military need **[unverified]**.
  - Henry Maudslay's screw-cutting lathe, about 1800: lead screw geared to the spindle, slide rest and change gears in one practical machine, giving accurate, repeatable threads and so interchangeable nuts and bolts. He did not invent the parts: Vaucanson had a slide-rest lathe about 1751 and Ramsden a screw-cutting lathe in 1775 — [Science Museum Group](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co46284/); [Wikipedia: Screw-cutting lathe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Screw-cutting_lathe).
  - This is the step everything later rests on: machines that make more accurate machines **[inference]**.

- **Spinning machines and factories.**
  - Hargreaves's jenny multiplied a hand spinner's output; Arkwright patented the water frame in 1769 and built the first water-powered cotton mill at Cromford, Derbyshire, in 1771, employing 200 people; the frame made stronger yarn and could be run by unskilled workers — [Wikipedia: Water frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_frame); [Wikipedia: Cromford Mill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cromford_Mill); [Derwent Valley Mills](https://www.derwentvalleymills.org/discover/derwent-valley-mills-history/derwent-valley-mills-history-development/).
  - The first factories ran on **water**, not steam: a river with fall is the gate **[inference]** from the Cromford facts.
  - Why Britain: high wages made labour-saving spinning machines pay (Allen); disputed by Humphries, who questions the high-wage evidence — [Allen 2013 restatement](https://www.nuff.ox.ac.uk/Economics/History/Paper115/allen115.pdf); [Reviews in History](https://reviews.history.ac.uk/review/943/print).

- **Railways.** No source retrieved this session; see Gaps.

- **Telegraph.** Cooke and Wheatstone patent, June 1837; first demonstrated 25 July 1837 on the London and Birmingham Railway between Euston and Camden Town; railway signalling was its first purpose; the Great Western installed a line in 1839. First US line 1844 — [Science Museum Group Journal](https://journal.sciencemuseum.ac.uk/article/cooke-and-wheatstones); [Wikipedia: Cooke and Wheatstone telegraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooke_and_Wheatstone_telegraph). Railways created the need (trains outran any messenger) and supplied the wayleave **[inference]**. Needs first: batteries, drawn copper wire, electromagnetism **[unverified]**.

#### F. Electricity, oil, chemistry

- **Electricity generation and grids.**
  - Faraday, 1831: electromagnetic induction and the first dynamo (a copper disc between magnet poles) — [APS News](https://www.aps.org/apsnews/2001/08/faraday-electromagnetism); [ETHW](https://www.ethw.org/Dynamo).
  - Practical generators came in the 1860s–70s, first for arc lighting. Edison's Pearl Street station, New York, 1882: about 400 lamps and 85 customers; direct current reached only about a mile — [National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/history-magazine/article/edison-tesla-current-war-ushered-electric-age); [Edison Tech Center](https://edisontechcenter.org/hist-elect-pow-trans/).
  - Alternating current with transformers and Tesla's induction motor (1887) allowed distance; Niagara Falls (Adams station, 1895) sent power to Buffalo in 1896 — [Edison Tech Center](https://edisontechcenter.org/ac-power-history/).
  - Note the 50-year gap from principle (1831) to service (1882): the pull (lighting) and the supporting parts (lamp, wire, engines) had to arrive **[inference]**.
  - Ground: falling water (hydro) or coal.

- **Oil.**
  - Before oil: lamp oil came from whales; by the 1850s the United States used more than 10 million gallons of whale oil a year. Kerosene refining began in 1849; Drake drilled the first well in 1859 (Titusville, Pennsylvania **[place unverified this session]**); it soon ran dry and the industry moved on — [ASME](https://www.asme.org/topics-resources/content/drake’s-oil-well-started-a-revolution).
  - So oil was first wanted for **light**, not engines; petrol was a by-product until the motor car **[inference]**.
- **Internal combustion engine.** Four-stroke cycle described by Beau de Rochas, never built by him; Nikolaus Otto built it in 1876, working with Daimler and Maybach — [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/technology/gasoline-engine/Development-of-gasoline-engines).

- **Chemistry: synthetic nitrogen (Haber-Bosch).**
  - Pressure: fixed nitrogen for fertiliser came from Peruvian guano, then Chilean saltpetre, which supplied two-thirds of the world's fixed-nitrogen fertiliser until 1914. In 1898 William Crookes warned the deposits would run out and wheat-eating nations would starve: "It is the chemist who must come to the rescue" — [RSC Historical Group paper](https://www.rsc.org/globalassets/03-membership-community/connect-with-others/through-interests/interest-groups/historical/other-publications/occasional-paper-7.pdf); [Science History Institute](https://www.sciencehistory.org/stories/magazine/dirty-business/).
  - Answer: Haber's laboratory synthesis about 1909; Bosch at BASF scaled it up; Mittasch found a cheap iron catalyst; the Oppau plant made ammonia from September 1913 — [Wikipedia: History of the Haber process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Haber_process); [American Scientist](https://www.americanscientist.org/article/from-fertile-minds).
  - War: when the British blockade cut off Chilean nitrate, the plant switched to nitrate for explosives — [Science History Institute](https://www.sciencehistory.org/stories/magazine/dirty-business/).
  - Needs first: high-pressure steel vessels, compressors, cheap hydrogen (from coal), catalysis theory **[unverified]**. The high-pressure plant know-how is a direct ancestor of rocket-propellant and liquid-gas plant **[inference]**.

- **Aluminium.**
  - Hall (USA, 23 February 1886) and Héroult (France, patent 23 April 1886) independently found the same process within weeks: electrolysis of alumina dissolved in molten cryolite — [ACS landmark](https://www.acs.org/education/whatischemistry/landmarks/aluminumprocess.html); [Science History Institute](https://sciencehistory.org/historical-profile/paul-heroult-and-charles-m-hall).
  - Before 1886 aluminium was a semi-precious metal priced like silver; Hall's company moved to Niagara Falls for cheap hydro power and the price fell from about $2 a pound to 30 cents — [Science History Institute](https://www.sciencehistory.org/stories/magazine/aluminum-common-metal-uncommon-past/).
  - It was "a direct application of the then-new development of dynamos and principally of waterpower" — [EBSCO: Charles Martin Hall](https://ebsco.com/research-starters/history/charles-martin-hall). Alumina comes from bauxite by the Bayer process — [Wikipedia: Hall–Héroult process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hall%E2%80%93H%C3%A9roult_process).
  - Clean supply-led case: the want was old, two people did it the moment cheap current existed **[inference]**.

#### G. Flight, electronics, rockets, orbit

- **Powered flight.** Wright brothers, 1903: a four-cylinder inline petrol engine with a cast aluminium-alloy crankcase, 12–16 hp at about 180 lb — [Aircraft Engine Historical Society](https://enginehistory.org/Piston/Before1925/AcEngHx/AcEngHx.shtml). Flight needed a light engine, which needed petrol, the Otto cycle and cheap aluminium, all less than 30 years old **[inference]**.

- **Radar.** The cavity magnetron (Randall and Boot, Birmingham, 1940; an impractical Bell Labs design was patented in 1934) gave powerful short-wave radar; about 250,000 were made for the Allies — [IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/tech-history/dawn-of-electronics/from-world-war-ii-radar-to-microwave-popcorn-the-cavity-magnetron-was-there); [Imperial War Museum](https://www.iwm.org.uk/collections/item/object/30005502). War-driven.

- **Electronic computer.** ENIAC, University of Pennsylvania, built May 1943 to February 1946 under contract to the Army's Ballistic Research Laboratory, because the Army was months behind on artillery firing tables — [US Army Research Lab history](https://ftp.arl.mil/mike/comphist/eniac-story.html); [Computer History Museum](https://www.computerhistory.org/revolution/birth-of-the-computer/4/78h). War-driven.

- **Transistor.** Bell Labs, 16 December 1947. Motive: the telephone network's vacuum tubes and relays were unreliable, power-hungry and hot; research director Mervin Kelly sought a solid-state replacement in germanium or silicon — [Computer History Museum](https://www.computerhistory.org/siliconengine/invention-of-the-point-contact-transistor/); [IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/honoring-the-trailblazing-transistor). Demand-led inside a firm rich enough to fund basic research **[inference]**.

- **Liquid oxygen (a hidden prerequisite).** Carl von Linde achieved large-scale air liquefaction in 1895 and, from 1902, continuous separation of oxygen above 99% purity; Linde's subsidiary Heylandt supplied the German rocket programme — [Science History Institute](https://sciencehistory.org/historical-profile/carl-von-linde); [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/EBchecked/topic/341835/Carl-von-Linde); [Wikipedia: Linde plc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linde_plc). Linde came to it from refrigeration (brewing) **[unverified]**; the industrial pull was oxygen-using processes such as steelmaking — [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/EBchecked/topic/341835/Carl-von-Linde).

- **Liquid-fuel rocketry.**
  - Theory: Tsiolkovsky showed in articles from 1903 that the rocket is the vehicle for space; Goddard reached similar conclusions unaware of him — [EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/launching-first-liquid-fueled-rocket). (Independent duplication again.)
  - First flight: Goddard, Auburn, Massachusetts, 16 March 1926, petrol and liquid oxygen, 41 feet high in 2.5 seconds — [NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/history/95-years-ago-goddards-first-liquid-fueled-rocket/).
  - V-2 (A-4), Germany: fuel 75% ethanol and 25% water with liquid oxygen; two pumps on one steam turbine, the steam made by decomposing hydrogen peroxide with sodium permanganate; pump flow about 128 lb of alcohol and 159 lb of oxygen a second; gyroscopic guidance and rudders in the jet. The pump had to handle alcohol at room temperature, oxygen at −297°F and steam at 725°F in one casing — [National Museum of the US Air Force](https://www.nationalmuseum.af.mil/Visit/Museum-Exhibits/Fact-Sheets/Display/Article/195894/v-2-rocket/); [Smithsonian NASM turbopump](https://si.edu/object/rocket-engine-turbopump-and-accessories-v-2:nasm_A19771237000); [Aircraft Engine Historical Society](https://www.enginehistory.org/Rockets/RPE02/RPE02-2.shtml).
  - Its four key technologies: large liquid-fuel engines, supersonic aerodynamics, gyroscopic guidance, jet-vane control — [Wikipedia: V-2 rocket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V-2_rocket).
  - Cost and people: over 3,000 built; at least 10,000 prisoners died making them at Mittelwerk; Neufeld's estimate is that the programme cost roughly $500 million and burdened Germany about as much as the Manhattan Project (about $2.2 billion) burdened the United States — [Smithsonian NASM](https://airandspace.si.edu/stories/editorial/wonder-weapons-and-slave-labor); [National WWII Museum interview with Neufeld](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/wernher-von-braun-and-nazi-rocket-program-interview-michael-neufeld-phd-national-air). (The cost comparison reached me through a secondary discussion; treat as approximate.)
  - R-7, Soviet Union: ordered from Korolev's bureau on 20 May 1954 as an intercontinental missile; Soviet warheads were heavy (about 5 tons for the hydrogen bomb), which forced a rocket far larger than early American ones and able to orbit about 1.5 tons. First flight 15 May 1957, first success 21 August 1957 — [NASA, Siddiqi](https://www.nasa.gov/history/sputnik/siddiqi.html); [NASA, Harford](https://www.nasa.gov/history/sputnik/harford.html); [Britannica: R-7](https://britannica.com/topic/R-7); [RussianSpaceWeb](https://russianspaceweb.com/r7.html).

- **First satellite and first human in orbit.**
  - Tikhonravov's satellite report went to the government with Korolev's covering letter on 26 May 1954; an earlier mention of a satellite had been struck from a 1953 draft decree; missile specialists objected to the diversion — [NASA, Korolev's letter](https://www.nasa.gov/history/sputnik/russ1.html); [NASA Monograph 10](https://www.nasa.gov/history/monograph10/korspace.html).
  - Sputnik 1 was rushed into production after the August 1957 test and launched on an R-7 on 4 October 1957 — [Britannica](https://britannica.com/science/space-exploration/From-Sputnik-to-Apollo).
  - Vostok 1: Yuri Gagarin, 12 April 1961, one orbit, 108 minutes, spacecraft mass 4,725 kg; he ejected and landed by parachute — [Wikipedia: Vostok 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vostok_1); [Planetary Society](https://planetary.org/space-missions/vostok-1); [AIP Inside Science](https://www.aip.org/inside-science/yuri-gagarins-space-shot-heard-round-the-world).
  - Reading: orbit was a **by-product of a weapon**. The state paid for the missile; the satellite rode on spare lifting capacity, pushed by a few enthusiasts **[inference]** from the Siddiqi and Harford accounts.

- **Nuclear power (optional branch).** Chicago Pile-1, first self-sustaining chain reaction, 2 December 1942 (graphite blocks with uranium, no shielding or cooling); Obninsk, USSR, first grid-connected station, 27 June 1954, 5 MW electric — [University of Chicago](https://news.uchicago.edu/explainer/first-nuclear-reactor-explained); [Wikipedia: Obninsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obninsk_Nuclear_Power_Plant). Not on the path to orbit, but the **bomb** is what paid for the R-7 **[inference]**.

### Inferences
- Three kinds of trigger recur and could be three kinds of cause in a model: (1) a chronic want met when a last prerequisite arrives (aluminium, telescope, flight); (2) a local shortage or bottleneck (flooded pits, London wood prices, Chilean nitrate, whale oil); (3) a state at war or fearing war paying for something no market would (radar, ENIAC, V-2, R-7).
- Several "advances" are really a reordering of what a designer might assume: pottery before farming (East Asia), storage before farming (Jordan), factories before steam (Cromford), oil before engines (lamps), copper tools before the wheel.
- Each big step reused the previous step's workshop: potters' kilns to smelters; clock and instrument makers to machine tools; cannon boring to steam cylinders; high-pressure chemistry and air liquefaction to rocket propellants.

## 2. Demand-led or supply-led? The scholarly debate

### Takeaway
No serious scholar now holds that need alone produces invention. The live disagreement is over what tips a society from having a want to acting on it: population pressure (Boserup), relative prices (Allen), a culture and community of knowledge (Mokyr), sheer numbers of minds (Kremer), available natural endowment (Diamond), or the stock of earlier parts to combine (Arthur, Basalla). These are compatible more often than their authors admit.

### Cited Findings
- **Ester Boserup (1965), *The Conditions of Agricultural Growth*.** Reverses Malthus: population growth pushes farmers to intensify. Land use runs forest-fallow, bush-fallow, short-fallow, annual cropping, multi-cropping. Each step needs *more* labour per unit of food, so nobody adopts it until density forces them. Property rights change with intensification. — [EH.net review](https://eh.net/book_reviews/the-conditions-of-agricultural-growth-the-economics-of-agrarian-change-under-population-pressure/); [Wikipedia: Ester Boserup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ester_Boserup)
  - Critique: she treats population growth as given; under very high pressure the result may be stagnation ("involution"), not progress; thresholds exist — [Turner and Ali, PNAS 1996](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC26249).
- **Joel Mokyr, *The Lever of Riches* (1990) and *A Culture of Growth* (2016).** Distinguishes rare *macroinventions* (breakthroughs) from the stream of *microinventions* that make them work, plus thousands of anonymous improvers. Sustained growth needed "propositional knowledge" (why things work) and an "Industrial Enlightenment" in which savants and craftsmen shared a culture; Europe's political fragmentation and its Republic of Letters explain why Europe and not China. Mokyr shared the 2025 Nobel Prize in economics for this work. — [Mokyr 2017, "Bottom-up or top-down"](https://sites.northwestern.edu/jmokyr/files/2018/02/Bottom-up-or-top-down-2fp03ny.pdf); [Econlib](https://www.econlib.org/library/enc/bios/mokyr.html); [Princeton University Press](https://press.princeton.edu/index%2Ephp/node/48531)
  - This is a supply-side view: demand is always there; what varies is the capacity and willingness to invent.
- **George Basalla (1988), *The Evolution of Technology*.** Rejects "the mistaken belief that humans are driven to invent new artifacts in order to meet basic biological needs such as food, shelter, and defense", and rejects heroic lone inventors. Every new artefact descends from an earlier one; societies *select* among novelties. — [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/evolution-of-technology/72D7695EE9A2BAFB75EFE648D4222A03)
- **Jared Diamond (1997), *Guns, Germs, and Steel*.** Geography decides: 13 of the 14 large domesticable mammals were Eurasian, and Eurasia's east-west axis let crops and techniques spread along one climate band. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guns,_Germs,_and_Steel)
  - Critique: a quantitative test found the continental axis "did not uniformly dictate" cultural spread; historians say geography is not sufficient (McNeill) — [University of Texas](https://cns.utexas.edu/news/research/study-challenges-popular-concept-spread-cultural-innovations); [McNeill 2001](https://careercenter.apsva.us/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/2024/06/McNeill-WorldAccordingJared-2001.pdf).
- **Robert Allen (2009), *The British Industrial Revolution in Global Perspective*.** Britain had the highest wages and the cheapest energy in the world; that made coal-burning, labour-saving machines profitable there and nowhere else. The inventions were adopted "because labour was expensive and coal was cheap". — [Allen excerpt](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805218/68273/excerpt/9780521868273_excerpt.pdf); [Reviews in History](https://reviews.history.ac.uk/review/943/print)
  - Critique: Jane Humphries disputes the high-wage evidence; Allen replied in 2013 — [Allen 2013](https://www.nuff.ox.ac.uk/Economics/History/Paper115/allen115.pdf). Mokyr's counter is that prices explain adoption but not the ability to invent **[unverified as a direct quotation]**.
- **Michael Kremer (1993), "Population Growth and Technological Change: One Million B.C. to 1990".** Ideas are non-rival, so more people means more inventors; with Malthusian limits this predicts population growth proportional to population, which fits the long-run data. Among regions cut off from each other, those with larger starting populations progressed faster. — [QJE abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/qjecon/v108y1993i3p681-716..html); [paper](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/1993-kremer.pdf)
  - Related and contested: Henrich (2004) argued Tasmania lost techniques after sea-level rise cut its population off, because too few learners remained; Vaesen et al. (PNAS 2016) and a 2016 review say hunter-gatherer evidence does not support population size as the driver of complexity — [Henrich 2004](https://www2.psych.ubc.ca/~henrich/Website/Papers/HenrichTasmania.pdf); [Vaesen et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4843435); [Phil. Trans. review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4920298).
- **W. Brian Arthur (2009), *The Nature of Technology*.** Technologies "come into being as fresh combinations of what already exists"; components are themselves technologies; the whole "creates itself out of itself". Darwinian selection only acts once a combination exists. — [Arthur, Santa Fe Institute essay](https://sites.santafe.edu/~wbarthur/Papers/NewSci%20Essay.pdf); [STIAS lecture](https://stias.ac.za/2019/06/combinatorial-evolution-a-theory-of-evolution-for-technology)
- **Vaclav Smil.** Energy transitions take decades to centuries: coal took about a century to go from 10% to 64% of UK energy; coal was about 95% of world commercial primary energy in 1900 and oil passed it only in the mid-1960s; new sources are added on top of old ones more than they replace them. Progress is also a sequence of better prime movers (steam engine, steam turbine, internal combustion). — [Energy Institute](https://knowledge.energyinst.org/new-energy-world/article?id=138921); [Smil excerpt](https://is.muni.cz/el/fss/podzim2019/IRE109/um/Smill_133_149.txt); [Smil, Funcas](https://www.funcas.es/wp-content/uploads/Migracion/Articulos/FUNCAS_PE/009art03.pdf); [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/a-global-transition-to-renewable-energy-will-take-many-decades/)
- **Lewis Dartnell (2014), *The Knowledge*.** A thought experiment on what a survivor society must know to rebuild; covers food, shelter, transport, communication, record-keeping; holds that the scientific method is the single most valuable thing to preserve. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Knowledge:_How_to_Rebuild_Our_World_from_Scratch); [Times Higher Education](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/node/121060)

### Inferences
- The theories apply at different levels and can be stacked in a model: Diamond sets what a place *can ever* do; Arthur and Basalla set what is *currently possible* given parts in hand; Boserup and Allen set what is *worth doing now* given people, land, wages and fuel prices; Kremer and Mokyr set *how fast* new possibilities are found (number of minds, and how well they share what they know).
- "Necessity is not the mother of invention" (Basalla) and "necessity drives intensification" (Boserup) are not in conflict: Boserup's farmers mostly *adopt known* methods under pressure; they do not invent from nothing. Pressure selects from a shelf; the shelf is filled by other means.
- Simultaneous invention (aluminium 1886, telescope 1608, rocket theory 1903–1919) is the best evidence for the combination view: when the parts exist, several people assemble them.

## 3. The minimum dependency chain from a stone-age village to a satellite

### Takeaway
The unavoidable spine is: surplus food, then high-temperature fire, then iron, then precision machining, then large-scale energy, then industrial chemistry and electricity, then cryogenic oxygen with turbopumps and gyroscopic guidance, all paid for by a state. Bronze, coinage, gunpowder, windmills, aviation, transistors and nuclear power are historically important but arguably not strictly required.

### Cited Findings
The chain, with the evidence that each link was really needed by the next:

1. **Stored food surplus and settlement** — storage preceded domestication at Dhra' — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2009/06/090623150619.htm).
2. **Farming with intensification** (irrigation or plough and draught animals) to free specialists — [Boserup, EH.net](https://eh.net/book_reviews/the-conditions-of-agricultural-growth-the-economics-of-agrarian-change-under-population-pressure/).
3. **Kilns and charcoal** — pottery (20 ka) long precedes smelting (5000 BC) — [Science 2012](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012Sci...336.1696W/abstract); [Radivojević](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10134145/7/Radivojevic_Ch11_The%20Rise%20of%20Metallurgy%20in%20Eurasia.pdf).
4. **Copper tools** — needed for the carpentry of wheels — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/why-it-took-so-long-to-inv/).
5. **Records and number** (tokens, writing, arithmetic) to run stores, taxes and works — [Schmandt-Besserat](https://sites.utexas.edu/dsb/tokens/from-accounting-to-writing/).
6. **Iron** — [Erb-Satullo 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10814-019-09129-6).
7. **Water power, gearing** — [Wikipedia: Watermill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watermill).
8. **Paper and printing** so knowledge accumulates and spreads — [Britannica](https://britannica.com/topic/printing-publishing/The-invention-of-printing).
9. **Glass, lenses, instruments; a community that tests claims** — [Oxford HSM](https://www.cabinet.ox.ac.uk/telescope); [Econlib on Mokyr](https://www.econlib.org/library/enc/bios/mokyr.html).
10. **Fossil fuel and a heat engine** — Newcomen 1712, viable because it sat on a coal mine — [Wikipedia: Newcomen engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newcomen_atmospheric_engine).
11. **Coke-smelted iron in quantity** — Darby 1709 — [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Abraham-Darby).
12. **Precision machine tools** — Wilkinson's boring mill, Maudslay's lathe — [Science Museum Group](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co46284/).
13. **Cheap steel** — Bessemer 1856 — [World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/audioplayer/en/2-2206/).
14. **Electricity** — dynamo, then grids — [ETHW](https://www.ethw.org/Dynamo).
15. **Industrial chemistry at high pressure and with catalysts** — Haber-Bosch 1913 — [Wikipedia: History of the Haber process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Haber_process).
16. **Air liquefaction and liquid oxygen** — Linde 1895–1902; his subsidiary supplied the German rockets — [Science History Institute](https://sciencehistory.org/historical-profile/carl-von-linde); [Wikipedia: Linde plc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linde_plc).

19. **Gyroscopic guidance and control surfaces in the jet; supersonic aerodynamics** — [Wikipedia: V-2 rocket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V-2_rocket).
20. **Radio** for tracking and for the satellite's signal — implied by Sputnik's design; not separately sourced here.
21. **Staging and clustered engines at scale, state funding** — R-7, ordered as a missile in 1954, orbit in 1957 — [NASA, Siddiqi](https://www.nasa.gov/history/sputnik/siddiqi.html).
22. **Human in orbit** adds life support, re-entry protection and recovery — Vostok 1, 4,725 kg, 1961 — [Wikipedia: Vostok 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vostok_1).

Elapsed time for scale: stone tools 3.3 Ma; farming about 11,000 BP; smelting 5000 BC; iron general by 1000 BC; steam 1712; Sputnik 1957. The last link, from the first liquid-fuel flight (1926) to orbit (1957), took 31 years — [NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/history/95-years-ago-goddards-first-liquid-fueled-rocket/); [Britannica](https://britannica.com/science/space-exploration/From-Sputnik-to-Apollo).

### Inferences
What could plausibly be **skipped** (all inference; flagged where I lean on an unverified fact):
- **Tin bronze.** Iron ore is common and tin is rare; a society with good kilns could in principle go from copper to iron. Sub-Saharan Africa is usually said to have done so **[unverified]**.
- **Coinage.** Accounts, weights and credit preceded coins by millennia (tokens from 7500 BC, coins about 600 BC); a planned or credit economy could run without them.
- **Gunpowder.** Not in the liquid-rocket chain technically; historically it drove cannon boring, which gave Wilkinson's cylinders, so something else would have to pull precision boring.
- **Windmills, canals, sailing ships.** Regional substitutes for water power and land carriage. Some bulk transport is needed, but which kind depends on geography.
- **The piston steam engine as prime mover.** The essential things are a large energy source and rotary power. The first factories ran on water (Cromford), and electricity can come from hydro (Niagara). A land rich in falling water but poor in coal might go water wheel, then turbine, then dynamo. It would still need coal or charcoal as a *chemical* reducing agent for iron, and Smil's evidence is that such shifts are slow.
- **Aviation.** Rockets do not need aircraft. But the aircraft industry supplied light alloys, aerodynamics and wind tunnels; without it rocket builders must create these.
- **Aluminium.** Useful for light structure; steel tanks are possible **[unverified]**.
- **Transistors and electronic computers.** Sputnik-era electronics used vacuum tubes **[unverified]**; trajectories can be computed by hand and desk calculators, slowly.
- **Nuclear power.** Not needed. Historically, though, the heavy hydrogen bomb is why the R-7 was big enough to orbit anything.
- **Internal combustion.** Not needed for the rocket itself, but petroleum refining (for kerosene) or large-scale fermentation and distilling (for alcohol) is.

What **cannot** be skipped: surplus food; high-temperature furnaces; iron and steel; precision metal cutting; mathematics and written records; a way to copy knowledge cheaply; experimental science (the vacuum, gases, thermodynamics, electromagnetism); electricity; industrial gas separation; and an organisation able to spend on the scale of a great power's wartime programme.

### Gaps
- No source was found that lays out a formal minimum dependency graph to orbit; the list above is assembled from the individual histories.
- Sputnik's own hardware (mass 83.6 kg, radio transmitters, batteries) was not confirmed in this session; the search found no source for the mass figure.
- The R-7's propellants (liquid oxygen and kerosene), its strap-on booster layout and engine designer (Glushko) are standard facts that were not confirmed in this session.
- Materials for the rockets (aluminium alloys, stainless steel, graphite vanes), the role of welding, and of ball bearings and seals were not retrieved.
- Whether Dartnell or Smil discusses leapfrogging steam was not confirmed.

## 4. Invented many times or once: how inevitable is each advance?

### Takeaway
Farming, pottery, metallurgy and (per the standard account) writing arose several times without contact, so they are close to inevitable wherever conditions allow. The wheel-and-axle, the alphabet-free leap to movable-type printing, and the scientific-industrial takeoff happened effectively once, which marks them as contingent on rare combinations. In the industrial era, near-simultaneous independent invention becomes common, which says that once the parts exist the next step is close to inevitable.

### Cited Findings
- **Agriculture: many times.** At least 11 independent centres in the Old and New Worlds; Bellwood judges New Guinea's to have developed "without external stimulus" — [World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/article/1886/origins-of-world-agriculture/); [Bellwood](https://press-files.anu.edu.au/downloads/press/n2540/html/ch02.xhtml). Harlan's classic paper frames this as "centers and noncenters" — [Science 1971](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17745730/).
- **Pottery: several times.** Among East Asian foragers from 20 ka, and reports describe it emerging in several groups, some independently; whether East Asia had one origin or many is still argued — [Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/?p=3066519); [Science 2012](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012Sci...336.1696W/abstract).
- **Metallurgy: probably several times.** Belovode "revived the possibility of multiple, rather than single, origins of metallurgy in Eurasia" — [Radivojević et al.](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1470180/1/S0959774314001097a.pdf).
- **Iron: debated.** Independent invention in sub-Saharan Africa (Nok culture, Nigeria, perhaps from about 1000 BC) is claimed but the only source retrieved marks the claim as lacking citation — [Wikipedia: Iron Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Age).
- **Wheel: probably once.** Anthony: "probably happened only once, in one place"; the 2024 study's authors allow multiple origins; Mesoamerica had the wheel on toys but never used it for transport — [Georgia Tech](https://ae.gatech.edu/news/2024/11/engineering-origin-wheel); [JSTOR Daily](https://daily.jstor.org/how-was-the-wheel-invented).
- **Coinage: Lydia, about 600 BC** in the Western tradition — [World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/article/797/the-invention-of-the-first-coinage-in-ancient-lydia/).
- **Windmill: twice in different forms.** Vertical-axis in Persia; horizontal-axis post mill in 12th-century north-west Europe — [Britannica](https://britannica.com/technology/windmill); [Mills Archive](https://new.millsarchive.org/2003/01/01/historical-introduction-to-mills-and-milling/3/).
- **Mechanical clock: China and Europe** by different mechanisms (Su Song 1092; European escapement about 1272) — [EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/science-and-technology-song-dynasty).
- **Gunpowder, paper, compass: once, in China**, then diffused — [Wikipedia: Four Great Inventions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Great_Inventions).
- **Telescope: several claimants within weeks, 1608** — [Oxford HSM](https://www.cabinet.ox.ac.uk/telescope).
- **Aluminium smelting: twice in two months, 1886** — [ACS](https://www.acs.org/education/whatischemistry/landmarks/aluminumprocess.html).
- **Rocket theory: Tsiolkovsky and Goddard independently** — [EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/launching-first-liquid-fueled-rocket).
- **Cavity magnetron: a 1934 Bell Labs patent preceded the 1940 British device** — [IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/tech-history/dawn-of-electronics/from-world-war-ii-radar-to-microwave-popcorn-the-cavity-magnetron-was-there).
- **Lathe: Vaucanson, Ramsden, Maudslay converged** on the same elements — [Wikipedia: Screw-cutting lathe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Screw-cutting_lathe).
- **Steam power and sustained industrial growth: once** (Britain, 18th century); Allen and Mokyr disagree about why, but both treat it as needing a rare conjunction — [Allen excerpt](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805218/68273/excerpt/9780521868273_excerpt.pdf); [Econlib on Mokyr](https://www.econlib.org/library/enc/bios/mokyr.html).

### Inferences
- A workable inevitability scale for a designer:
  - *Near-certain given conditions*: fire use, stone tools, clothing in cold, storage, pottery, farming where domesticable species exist, copper where ores and kilns coexist, some form of record-keeping once institutions hold stores.
  - *Likely but slow, and geography-dependent*: bronze (tin), iron, the wheel (draught animals and flat land), sail, water mills, writing.
  - *Rare conjunctions, happened once*: gunpowder, printing with cast type, the experimental-science community, fossil-fuelled industrialisation.
  - *Near-certain once the industrial base exists*: dynamo, telegraph, aluminium, internal combustion, flight, radio, rockets. After about 1800, duplication is the rule.
- The asymmetry suggests that the hard, contingent gates are few (roughly: metallurgy to iron, a knowledge-sharing culture, and the coal-and-engine takeoff) and everything between them tends to follow.

### Gaps
- Writing's independent origins were not sourced this session (see section 1 Gaps).
- Independent metallurgy in the Americas (Andean copper) was searched for and nothing was returned.
- African iron independence needs a peer-reviewed archaeometallurgy source.
- I found no source that systematically counts independent inventions across all these technologies.

### Inferences
- There is a clear historical trend a model can use: late technologies **dissolve** earlier geographic gates. Haber-Bosch replaces a desert in Chile with air and energy; Linde gets oxygen from air; electricity moves water power away from the river bank. Energy becomes the universal substitute for location.
- Co-location matters more than mere presence. Coal mattered where it lay beside iron ore and water transport; hydro mattered where a smelter could be built beside it.
- Tin is the best ancient example of a resource that forces trade and therefore ships, routes and trust; a region with copper but no tin and no trade stays in the copper age or jumps to iron.

- Uranium geography and enrichment prerequisites were not returned by the search.

## 6. Advances driven by a shortage or crisis

### Takeaway
A shortage rarely creates a technique from nothing; it makes an already-known but more troublesome alternative worth adopting (coal for wood, iron for bronze, coke for charcoal, kerosene for whale oil, synthetic for mined nitrate). War acts the same way but with the state as the buyer.

### Cited Findings
- **Bronze trade collapse to iron (about 1200–1000 BC).** Shortage hypothesis: loss of copper and tin supply pushed adoption of iron, which had been known and little used for 800 years; now seen as consequence of the collapse — [Erb-Satullo 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10814-019-09129-6).
- **Wood to coal (England, 1550–1700).** Nef's "timber crisis"; contested; Allen's version is a London price effect giving coal about a 50% discount — [Berkeley Economic Review](https://econreview.studentorg.berkeley.edu/the-roots-of-modernity-energy-crises-industrialization-and-technological-change/); [Steinmueller 2013](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/respol/v42y2013i10p1739-1748.html).
- **Flooded mines to the steam pump (1698–1712).** Deeper pits filled with water; Savery, then Newcomen — [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Thomas-Newcomen); [APS News](https://aps.org/apsnews/2018/07/savery-steam-engine).
- **Charcoal to coke for iron (1709).** Darby — [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Abraham-Darby). (That a charcoal shortage motivated it is the traditional reading; not separately sourced.)
- **Dear labour to spinning machines (1760s–70s).** Allen's high-wage argument — [Allen excerpt](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805218/68273/excerpt/9780521868273_excerpt.pdf).
- **Whale oil to kerosene and petroleum (1849–1859).** US whale-oil use above 10 million gallons a year in the 1850s — [ASME](https://www.asme.org/topics-resources/content/drake’s-oil-well-started-a-revolution).
- **Guano and Chilean nitrate to synthetic ammonia (1898–1913).** Crookes's warning; Chile supplied two-thirds of fixed nitrogen to 1914 — [RSC](https://www.rsc.org/globalassets/03-membership-community/connect-with-others/through-interests/interest-groups/historical/other-publications/occasional-paper-7.pdf).
- **Blockade to nitrate for explosives (1914).** Oppau switched from fertiliser to munitions — [Science History Institute](https://www.sciencehistory.org/stories/magazine/dirty-business/).
- **War to radar (1940).** About 250,000 magnetrons — [IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/tech-history/dawn-of-electronics/from-world-war-ii-radar-to-microwave-popcorn-the-cavity-magnetron-was-there).
- **War to the electronic computer (1943–46).** Backlog of firing tables — [US Army Research Lab](https://ftp.arl.mil/mike/comphist/eniac-story.html).
- **War to large rockets (1930s–45).** V-2; programme cost comparable in burden to the Manhattan Project — [National WWII Museum](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/wernher-von-braun-and-nazi-rocket-program-interview-michael-neufeld-phd-national-air).
- **Cold War to the R-7 and so to Sputnik (1954–57).** Missile ordered for a heavy warhead; satellite added on — [NASA, Siddiqi](https://www.nasa.gov/history/sputnik/siddiqi.html); [NASA, Harford](https://www.nasa.gov/history/sputnik/harford.html).
- **Rivalry to human spaceflight.** "Sputnik gave birth to NASA and Gagarin's flight gave birth to Apollo" (John Logsdon) — [AIP Inside Science](https://www.aip.org/inside-science/yuri-gagarins-space-shot-heard-round-the-world).
- **Unreliable tubes to the transistor (1947).** A firm's internal bottleneck — [Computer History Museum](https://www.computerhistory.org/siliconengine/invention-of-the-point-contact-transistor/).
- **Boserup's general case:** land shortage (shorter fallows) to hoe, plough, manure, irrigation — [EH.net](https://eh.net/book_reviews/the-conditions-of-agricultural-growth-the-economics-of-agrarian-change-under-population-pressure/).
- **Counter-case:** pressure can instead produce stagnation; Turner and Ali find thresholds beyond which Bangladesh's farmers did not intensify further — [PNAS 1996](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC26249).

### Inferences
- In almost every case the substitute was **already known** and was worse in some way (coal smokes and needs chimneys; iron is harder to work than bronze; the Newcomen engine wastes fuel). The shortage changed the price, not the knowledge. A cause-driven model should therefore keep "known but not worth it" as a distinct state from "unknown".
- The shortage is usually **local** (London, one mining district, one blockaded country), and the solution then spreads to places that never had the shortage.
- Shortage with no alternative on the shelf gives decline, not invention (Boserup's critics; the Late Bronze Age collapse itself).

### Gaps
- The "timber famine" literature was reached only through a student-written summary and one abstract; Nef, Flinn, Hammersley, Allen 2003 and Warde were not read directly.
- Whether Darby's move to coke was shortage-driven or cost-driven was not sourced.
- The British wartime wood, rubber and oil substitutions (synthetic rubber, coal-to-oil) were not researched.

## 7. How many people and how much surplus each stage needed

### Takeaway
Direct figures are scarce and I found no source giving a minimum population per technology. What can be sourced: pre-industrial societies kept roughly 80–90% of people on the land; the first industrial nation had already cut that below half by 1700, *before* steam mattered; and the final step to orbit took programmes of tens to hundreds of thousands of workers inside states of over a hundred million.

### Cited Findings
- **Bands and hamlets** made stone tools, fire, clothing and even pottery: Xianrendong's potters were foragers — [Science 2012](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012Sci...336.1696W/abstract).
- **A small village** built the first granaries: Dhra' had stores about 3 m across, each used for no more than about 50 years — [Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/ancient-granaries-preceded-agricultural-revolution).
- **A village could smelt copper:** Belovode is a single Vinča settlement, and the earliest evidence is about 5 g of slag — [Radivojević](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10134145/7/Radivojevic_Ch11_The%20Rise%20of%20Metallurgy%20in%20Eurasia.pdf). Smelting starts as a part-time craft **[inference]**.
- **Bronze needed an inter-regional system:** tens of tons of tin a year moving from Britain to the eastern Mediterranean — [Durham University](https://dur.ac.uk/news-events/latest-news/2025/05/britains-long-distance-tin-trade-transformed-the-bronze-age/).
- **Writing needed an institution with pooled surplus** (the redistribution economy the tokens served) — [Schmandt-Besserat](https://sites.utexas.edu/dsb/tokens/tokens-and-writing-the-cognitive-development/).
- **Urban share, ancient and medieval:** Europe about 11% urban around 200 CE at the Roman peak; over 10% marks the more developed pre-industrial societies; Rome about one million people around 100 CE, falling to about 50,000 by 800; medieval Italy reached 15–16% — [Jedwab et al., CEPR DP14828](https://repec.cepr.org/repec/cpr/ceprdp/DP14828.pdf); [WSU open text](https://opentext.wsu.edu/introtohumangeography/?p=813).
- **Europe 14th–19th centuries:** fewer than 10% lived in towns over 10,000 — [Statista summary](https://www.statista.com/statistics/1304890/urbanization-europe-1800/). Europe excluding Russia was about 87.7% rural in 1700 — [MIT table](https://web.mit.edu/course/4/4.291/www/SPURS/tables102.htm).
- **England, the outlier:** share of the male labour force in agriculture fell from 68% in 1522 to 46% about 1700 (47% in 1701 on another estimate); people in towns over 5,000 rose from 5% in 1500 to 28% in 1800 — [LSE, Structural change 1500–1800](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/84510); [Cambridge Group blog](https://www.campop.geog.cam.ac.uk/blog/2024/12/12/agriculture/); [Clark, UC Davis](https://faculty.econ.ucdavis.edu/faculty/gclark/papers/How%20rich%20-%20final.pdf).
- **Long-run data source:** Our World in Data's agricultural employment series uses Broadberry and Gardner for 1300–1800 benchmarks, for a few countries only — [OWID sources note](https://ourworldindata.org/agri-employment-sources).
- **First factory:** Cromford mill, 200 employees in 1771 — [Derwent Valley Mills](https://www.derwentvalleymills.org/discover/derwent-valley-mills-history/derwent-valley-mills-history-development/).
- **First power station:** 85 customers, 400 lamps, one square mile — [National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/history-magazine/article/edison-tesla-current-war-ushered-electric-age).
- **Big-science scale, 1940s:** Manhattan Project peak about 129,000 workers in 1944 and about $2.2 billion; V-2 production used about 60,000 forced labourers at Mittelwerk plus the Peenemünde staff (figures from a secondary discussion citing the US Army history and Neufeld; treat as approximate) — [Wikipedia talk page: V-2 rocket](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Talk:V-2); deaths at Mittelwerk at least 10,000 — [Smithsonian NASM](https://airandspace.si.edu/stories/editorial/wonder-weapons-and-slave-labor).
- **Numbers of minds:** Kremer's finding that isolated regions with bigger populations advanced faster; Henrich's Tasmanian case of loss in a small isolated population; both contested for foragers — [Kremer 1993](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/qjecon/v108y1993i3p681-716..html); [Henrich 2004](https://www2.psych.ubc.ca/~henrich/Website/Papers/HenrichTasmania.pdf); [Vaesen et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4843435).
- **Boserup on density:** each farming system belongs to a density range; intensification is not worth its labour below it — [EH.net](https://eh.net/book_reviews/the-conditions-of-agricultural-growth-the-economics-of-agrarian-change-under-population-pressure/).

### Inferences
An order-of-magnitude ladder, as a starting point for design. **None of these thresholds is from a source; they are my estimates from the facts above and should be presented as such.**

| Stage | Smallest unit that has historically sustained it | Non-farming share |
|---|---|---|
| Fire, stone tools, hafting, clothing, pottery | Band of tens, in contact with others | None; everyone forages |
| Storage, farming, weaving | Village of about 100–1,000 | Near zero; part-time crafts |
| Copper smelting, the ard, irrigation ditches | Village or cluster of villages, low thousands | A few part-time specialists |
| Bronze, writing, large irrigation, wheeled transport, sail | City-state and its trade partners, tens of thousands and up | About 5–10% |
| Iron, coinage, mills, concrete, roads | Kingdom or empire, hundreds of thousands to millions | About 10% urban (Roman peak 11%) |
| Paper, printing, clocks, lenses, science | A connected civilisation of many millions with a shared scholarly language | 10–15% |
| Coal, steam, coke iron, factories | A nation of several million with half its workers already off the land (England 1700: about 46% in farming, population not sourced here) | About 50% |
| Steel, rail, telegraph, electricity, industrial chemistry | Industrial nations of tens of millions, trading globally | Majority non-farm |
| Rockets to orbit, nuclear | A great power of 100 million or more, able to put on the order of 100,000 people and a wartime-scale budget on one project | Large majority non-farm |

- The share off the land matters more than head count: England moved its farm share from 68% to 46% between 1522 and 1700 and industrialised; much larger agrarian empires did not.
- A small settlement can *use* and even *make* much of this if it trades with a larger system. What it cannot do alone is hold all the specialisms. The question for a model is the size of the connected network, not of the village.
- Both Kremer and his critics can be respected: population size raises the rate of discovery only if people are in contact and knowledge is retained (Mokyr's point, Henrich's Tasmanian point).

### Gaps
- No source found gives a minimum viable population for an iron industry, a steel industry or a space programme. The widely quoted estimates of this kind are speculative and I did not find a reliable one.
- Populations of England in 1700, Germany in 1939–45 and the USSR in 1957, and the size of the Soviet missile workforce, were not retrieved.
- Shares of non-farmers in Mesopotamian, Egyptian or Chinese states were not retrieved; the urban figures above are European.
- The Manhattan and Mittelwerk workforce figures come via a Wikipedia talk page, which is a poor source; they should be re-checked against the US Army official history and Neufeld's *The Rocket and the Reich*.
- Our World in Data's exact values for 1700 and 1800 were not read.
